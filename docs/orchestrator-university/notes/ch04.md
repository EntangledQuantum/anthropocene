# Chapter 4 — Newton's Laws: research notes

Notes taken while writing `content/paths/university-physics/04-interaction/`. Everything
here fed either a misconception entry in a concept file or a wrong option in a `<Predict>`.

## The Force Concept Inventory taxonomy (Hestenes, Wells & Swackhamer 1992)

The FCI decomposes Newtonian force into six dimensions, each with named
sub-misconceptions. The two clusters that own Chapter 4:

**Active Force (AF)** — the "motion requires a force" family, and the single most
documented wrong belief in physics education:

- AF1 only active agents exert forces (a table cannot *do* anything, so it exerts nothing)
- AF2 motion implies an active force
- AF3 no motion implies no force
- AF4 velocity is proportional to the applied force

AF4 is the sneaky one and the one worth designing for. A learner who has been told "no
force is needed to keep it moving" will still often predict that *adding* a forward force
to a coasting object produces a **new constant, higher speed** rather than steady
acceleration. That is the Aristotelian model surviving verbal correction intact, and it is
only falsifiable by watching the speed keep climbing. It is the reason `FbdBuilder` has to
let the learner actually place that forward arrow and run the world.

**Impetus (I)** — motion carries a stuff inside it:

- I1 impetus supplied by a hit
- I2 loss / recovery of original impetus
- I3 impetus dissipation
- I4 gradual or delayed impetus build-up
- I5 circular impetus

Practically, impetus shows up as "force" being used interchangeably with momentum or with
energy. Named in `force-as-interaction.yaml` as `force-is-a-possession`.

Also flagged repeatedly in the literature as *the* most fundamental confusion: **zero
velocity implies zero acceleration and therefore zero net force.** Chapter 2 already owns
`zero-velocity-zero-acceleration`; Chapter 4 links to it rather than re-teaching, and
Lesson 1's `<Classify>` beat is built from it (a body at rest *starting* to move is
perfectly consistent with a steady net force).

Sources: [Hestenes, Wells & Swackhamer, *Force Concept Inventory*, The Physics Teacher
30 (1992)](https://gwern.net/doc/science/physics/1992-hestenes.pdf) ·
[Modified module analysis of FCI misconception structure,
arXiv:1905.06176](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1905.06176) ·

## Third-law items — what specifically breaks

FCI items 4 and 28 are the car/truck problems. The literature is blunt: a **majority** of
students say the truck exerts the larger force on the car, and the belief survives
instruction. Two things are going on, and they need separating in the lesson:

1. **Force is being read off the damage.** The reasoning is "you would rather be in the
   truck, so the car takes the bigger hit." The observation is correct; it is an
   observation about *acceleration*, which differs by the mass ratio, not about force.
2. **The third law is believed to be a special case** that holds when masses are similar
   and fails when they are not. So the lesson must run the *asymmetric* case, not a
   symmetric one — a symmetric collision makes equal forces look like a coincidence of
   equal masses.

The design consequence: show equal force magnitudes and grossly unequal accelerations **in
the same frame, from the same simulation**, with both numbers printed. A 1200 kg car and a
14 000 kg truck give an acceleration ratio of about 11.7, which is large enough to read off
the screen.

Sources: [What do student responses to the car-truck problems tell us?
arXiv:2609.01477](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.01477) ·
[Fluctuations in student understanding of Newton's 3rd law,
arXiv:1009.0260](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1009.0260)

## Weight and the normal force

A microteaching study found ~95% of participants carried at least one of: weight and normal
force are a third-law pair; the normal force is "the reaction to gravity"; the normal force
is always equal to $mg$.

The diagnostic that separates these cleanly is *not* the book on the table (where the
numbers agree and every wrong model gets the right answer). It is:

- pull up on the block and watch $N$ **shrink**, then clamp at zero — a surface pushes and
  never pulls;
- and the observation that gravity's genuine partner is the pull the block exerts on the
  **Earth**, which can never appear on the block's own diagram.

Encoded as `normal-force-is-gravity-partner`, `normal-always-mg` and
`pair-forces-cancel` in `normal-force.yaml` / `third-law-pair.yaml`.

Source: [Misconception of weights, normal forces and Newton's third law, J. Phys. Conf.
Ser. 1572 012046](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/1742-6596/1572/1/012046/pdf)

## Free-body-diagram instruction — the finding that shaped the widget

The result worth building around: **handing students a completed free-body diagram inside
a problem statement did not help and measurably hurt.** Students who *construct* their own
diagrams outperform; students prompted to use a supplied one drift into rote method and do
worse. Separately, audits of student-drawn FBDs find ~30% omit axes entirely and ~18% get a
force direction wrong.

So `FbdBuilder` is a *builder* first and a figure second: the default mode has the learner
placing and scaling the arrows, the derived net-force and acceleration arrows are not
editable, and the axes and a newton scale bar are always drawn. The read-only `given` mode
exists only for the payoff panel inside a `<Predict>`, where the commitment has already
been made.

Sources: [Do students use and understand free-body diagrams? Phys. Rev. ST PER 5,
010108](https://link.aps.org/doi/10.1103/PhysRevSTPER.5.010108) ·
[Free-body diagrams and problem solving in mechanics, EJPE
2016](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1174568) ·
[Analyzing students' skill in drawing a free-body
diagram](https://www.academia.edu/100617542/Analyzing_students_skill_in_drawing_a_free_body_diagram)

## Design decisions that came out of the above

- The learner's arrows are the only editable objects. Net force and acceleration are
  derived, drawn in a deliberately different visual language (dashed outline, distinct
  colour), and cannot be dragged. The claim "the sum sets the acceleration" is therefore
  structural, not prose.
- The world pane can be **hidden** (`hideMotion`). That is the curriculum's visual question
  implemented literally, and it makes the payoff of Lesson 1's `<Classify>` beat possible:
  from the diagram you can state $\vec a$ and you cannot state $\vec v$.
- Third-law partners are drawn *outside* the dashed system boundary, on a ghost of the
  other body, and `netForceOn` in `src/lib/physics/dynamics.ts` filters by `on`, so a
  partner force is incapable of entering the sum. Same trick as `LinkedGraphs` making
  velocity the only editable curve.
- The asymmetric collision, not the symmetric one.
- The normal force is solved by the physics with a `max(0, …)` clamp rather than drawn by
  the learner, so "the table stops pushing and the block leaves" is something that happens
  to them rather than something they are told.
