# Chapter 1 — Language of Nature: research notes

Working notes for the two flagship lessons (dimensional balance, and the arrow that
survives a change of grid). Everything load-bearing here is either checked against a
source below or asserted in `src/lib/physics/__tests__/dimensions.test.ts`.

## SI since 20 May 2019

All seven base units are now defined by fixing exact numerical values for seven defining
constants. Nothing is a physical artefact any more; the IPK is retired.

| Constant | Exact value | Fixes |
|---|---|---|
| Δν(¹³³Cs) hyperfine | 9 192 631 770 Hz | the second |
| c | 299 792 458 m/s | the metre |
| h | 6.626 070 15 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s | the kilogram |
| e | 1.602 176 634 × 10⁻¹⁹ C | the ampere |
| k | 1.380 649 × 10⁻²³ J/K | the kelvin |
| N_A | 6.022 140 76 × 10²³ mol⁻¹ | the mole |
| K_cd | 683 lm/W | the candela |

Consequences worth using in a lesson: `1 eV = 1.602176634e-19 J` is now **exact**, and the
kilogram is reachable from a frequency measurement because `h` has dimension M L² T⁻¹ —
so fixing `h`, `c` and `Δν` pins a mass. That is the honest reason the redefinition is a
statement about dimensions rather than about better weighing.

Source: [2019 revision of the SI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2019_revision_of_the_SI).

## The seven base dimensions (ISQ)

Order used in code: **L, M, T, I, Θ, N, J** — length, mass, time, electric current,
thermodynamic temperature, amount of substance, luminous intensity. A physical quantity is
a point in that 7-space of exponents; multiplying quantities adds the vectors. This is the
structure the first lesson is built on, and it is why `Dimension` is a 7-tuple in
`src/lib/physics/dimensions.ts`.

## Mars Climate Orbiter (the hook for lesson 1)

Lost on 23 September 1999. The ground-side "Small Forces" software reported thruster
impulse in **pound-force seconds**; the trajectory software consumed the numbers as
**newton seconds**. Both are impulse — the *dimensions agreed perfectly*. Only the unit
differed, by a factor of 4.448 221 615 260 5 (= 0.45359237 kg × 9.80665 m/s² exactly).

This is the pedagogically valuable part: a dimensional check would **not** have caught it.
Dimension answers "is this the same kind of thing"; the unit answers "how much of it". The
lesson needs both halves for exactly this reason.

## What dimensional analysis cannot do

- It is blind to dimensionless factors: `√(L/g)` and `2π√(L/g)` are the same point in
  exponent space. The period question is settled *up to* 2π and no further.
- It is blind to sign, and blind to which dimensionless group you are in (Re, Ma, angle in
  degrees vs radians).
- It is blind to a wrong unit inside a correct dimension — see Mars above.
- It *is* strong enough to be predictive: with only L, g and m available, there is exactly
  one exponent combination with the dimension of time, and the mass exponent in it is
  zero. The pendulum period cannot depend on mass. That claim is brute-forced and asserted
  in the tests rather than told to the learner.

## Vector misconceptions (lesson 2)

Physics-education findings that shaped the solvables:

- **Coordinate-system "movability".** Students routinely fail to appreciate that the axes
  are a *choice they can move*, and treat them as a property of the situation. Getting two
  readouts to behave at once requires understanding what the readouts mean relative to the
  axes. ([Eur. J. Phys. 41, 2020](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/1361-6404/ab8b54))
- **Component items are the hardest vector items**, harder than direction; a documented
  error is believing a component can have the same magnitude as the vector itself.
  ([Science and engineering students' difficulties in understanding vector concepts](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/372236994_Science_and_engineering_students'_difficulties_in_understanding_vector_concepts))
- Tip-to-tip instead of tail-to-tip addition, and treating the dot product as
  "multiplication of vectors", both appear in the same literature.

Design response: the learner rotates **the grid, not the arrow**, and the widget prints two
columns — what changed and what did not — with the invariants recomputed *from the rotated
components* so the numbers are the ones a learner would have written down. The drift is
then genuinely 10⁻¹⁶, not a hardcoded zero.

## Numbers used in the lessons (all exact, all computed in `dimensions.ts`)

- 1 mi = 1609.344 m · 1 ft = 0.3048 m · 1 in = 0.0254 m · 1 furlong = 201.168 m
- 1 h = 3600 s · 1 fortnight = 1 209 600 s
- 1 lb = 0.453 592 37 kg · g₀ = 9.806 65 m/s² · 1 lbf = 4.448 221 615 260 5 N
- 1 mph = 0.447 04 m/s · 1 knot = 1852/3600 m/s
- 1 furlong per fortnight = 201.168 / 1 209 600 = 1.6631… × 10⁻⁴ m/s

Composite units are *computed from their primitives* in the registry (mph from mi and h,
psi from lbf and in, hp from ft, lbf and s), so a test can assert the composition instead
of trusting a typed constant.
