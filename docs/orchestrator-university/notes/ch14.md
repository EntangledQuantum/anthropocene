## 1. The large-angle pendulum

The exact period of a simple pendulum released from rest at $\theta_0$ is

$$T(\theta_0) = 4\sqrt{L/g}\; K\!\left(\sin^2 \tfrac{\theta_0}{2}\right),$$

with $K(m)$ the complete elliptic integral of the first kind in the **parameter**
convention $m = k^2$. The series expansion is

$$\frac{T}{T_0} = 1 + \frac{\theta_0^2}{16} + \frac{11\,\theta_0^4}{3072} + \cdots$$

`ellipticK` uses the arithmetic–geometric mean: $K(m) = \pi / (2\,\mathrm{AGM}(1,\sqrt{1-m}))$.
The AGM converges quadratically, so ~6 iterations is machine precision away from
$\theta_0 = \pi$, where $K$ diverges logarithmically (a pendulum balanced exactly at the top
never comes back — that is the divergence, and it is physical).

Numbers the lessons use, all produced by `pendulumPeriod`:

| $\theta_0$ | $T/T_0$ | what it means |
|---|---|---|
| 5° | 1.00048 | a clock losing 4 s a day if it drifts to 0° |
| 23.0° | 1.0100 | the **1 % threshold** — surprisingly small |
| 45° | 1.0400 | |
| 90° | 1.1803 | |
| 170° | 3.0 (2.999) | |
| 179° | 4.34 | |

The 1 % threshold is the `<Tune>` target in lesson 1. The series says
$\theta_0 \approx 4\sqrt{0.01} = 0.4$ rad $= 22.9°$, and the exact bisection gives 22.96°,
so the cheap expansion is already right to two figures there. Worth knowing, not worth
saying in the lesson.

The `pendulum` landscape in `landscape.ts` is $U = 4(1-\cos\theta)$ at unit mass, i.e.
$g/L = 4$, so $T_0 = \pi$ and $T(\theta_0) = 2K(\sin^2\tfrac{\theta_0}{2})$. The test
cross-checks the **Verlet-measured** period against the **AGM elliptic** period — two
completely independent computations — and they agree to better than 0.2 % out to 150°.

## 2. The driven damped oscillator

Convention used throughout: $\ddot x + 2\beta\dot x + \omega_0^2 x = f_0\cos\omega t$, with
$f_0 = F_0/m$. (Many texts write $\gamma = 2\beta$; the module exposes $\beta$ and derives
everything else, so the two never get mixed.)

$$A(\omega) = \frac{f_0}{\sqrt{(\omega_0^2-\omega^2)^2 + 4\beta^2\omega^2}},\qquad
\delta(\omega) = \operatorname{atan2}\!\left(2\beta\omega,\ \omega_0^2-\omega^2\right)$$

**Three different frequencies, and students conflate them.** This is the whole of the
curriculum's "damping changes $\omega_0$ in a way students cannot track":

| quantity | frequency | note |
|---|---|---|
| free ringing of the damped oscillator | $\sqrt{\omega_0^2-\beta^2}$ | what you'd measure with a stopwatch after one kick |
| peak of the amplitude response | $\sqrt{\omega_0^2-2\beta^2}$ | the visible peak of $A(\omega)$ |
| phase lag exactly 90°, **and** peak average power | $\omega_0$ | independent of $\beta$ |

All three collapse onto $\omega_0$ as $\beta \to 0$, which is why the distinction is
invisible in the light-damping pictures textbooks draw and then ambushes people.

**Power.** With $x = A\cos(\omega t - \delta)$ and $F = F_0\cos\omega t$,

$$\langle P\rangle = \tfrac12 F_0 A\omega \sin\delta = m\beta\omega^2A^2,$$

maximised exactly at $\omega = \omega_0$ for every $\beta$. At that point $\delta = 90°$, so
the drive is exactly in phase with the **velocity** — every push is with the motion, none
of it wasted pushing against the displacement. That is the "when do you push a swing"
answer, and it is the lesson's hook.

**Width.** Setting $\langle P\rangle = \tfrac12\langle P\rangle_{\max}$ gives
$4\beta^2\omega^2 = (\omega_0^2-\omega^2)^2$, so $\omega_\pm = \mp\beta + \sqrt{\omega_0^2+\beta^2}$
and the full width at half power is **exactly** $2\beta$, with no light-damping
approximation. Hence $Q = \omega_0/\Delta\omega = \omega_0/(2\beta)$ exactly for the power
curve. `halfPowerWidth` finds the two half-power frequencies by bisection on the computed
power curve; the test asserts the numerically-found width equals $2\beta$ to 1e-9, which
is an honest check that the closed form and the curve are the same object.

**Peak gain.** $A_{\max}/A_{\text{static}} = \omega_0^2 / (2\beta\sqrt{\omega_0^2-\beta^2})$,
which tends to $Q$ for light damping but is not equal to it. `betaForPeakGain` inverts this
by bisection; the `<Tune>` target of gain 5 at $\omega_0 = 1$ sits at $\beta = 0.100504$,
i.e. $Q = 4.975$, not 5. The lesson does not make a fuss of the difference — but the
number the widget grades against is the real root, not $\omega_0/(2\cdot 5)$.

**Resonance is not infinite.** The peak height is $f_0/(2\beta\sqrt{\omega_0^2-\beta^2})$:
finite for every $\beta > 0$, growing like $1/\beta$. It also takes longer to arrive — the
transient decays as $e^{-\beta t}$, so the time to reach steady state grows like $1/\beta$
too. Both halves matter, and the widget shows the second by running the actual transient
rather than drawing the steady-state envelope.

[Caltech Ph 12, *Damped and driven harmonic oscillator*](https://xiechen.caltech.edu/documents/30654/Ph12-lecture1007.pdf) ·
[TU Graz, *Quality factor*](http://lampz.tugraz.at/~hadley/physikm/script/oscillations/Q.en.php)

## 3. Documented student difficulties

From the SHM education-research literature, the ones the lessons are built to hit:

- **Period is read as "distance over speed".** A bigger swing is further, so it must take
  longer. The correct statement is that the restoring force grows in exact proportion to
  the displacement, so the extra distance is paid for by extra speed and the two cancel —
  and that cancellation is special to a *linear* restoring force, which is why the real
  pendulum does not do it.
- **Velocity and position are imagined in phase.** Students place maximum speed at maximum
  displacement. The circling-shadow picture fixes this by making $v$ the *horizontal
  component of a tangent vector*, which is visibly zero at the extremes.
- **Phase is treated as a time in seconds rather than an angle.** Hence the shadow widget
  puts $\varphi$ on the circle as an angle before it appears in $x = A\cos(\omega t + \varphi)$.
- **Restoring force imagined as constant** (gravity-like) rather than proportional to
  displacement.
- **Resonance imagined as unbounded**, and as "the response is in step with the drive".

Sources: [Investigating student understanding of simple harmonic motion](https://www.academia.edu/114128328/Investigating_student_understanding_of_simple_harmonic_motion) ·
[Overcoming students' misconceptions about simple harmonic oscillation through interactive conceptual instruction](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/337443782_Overcoming_students'_misconceptions_about_simple_harmonic_oscillation_through_interactive_conceptual_instruction)

## 4. What later chapters should reuse

`<ResonanceSweep>` is deliberately costume-neutral: the response quantity, the drive
quantity and the unit strings are props, and the physics is
$\ddot q + 2\beta\dot q + \omega_0^2 q = f_0\cos\omega t$ — which is the series RLC loop with
$\omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{LC}$, $2\beta = R/L$, $Q = \frac{1}{R}\sqrt{L/C}$. Chapters 30 and 31 should set
those props rather than build a second resonance widget. Chapter 40 gets the potential
track, which already exists.
