## Thirty kelvin each

Each flame gives 100 J every second. The meters count what went in and what left.

<Recall id="up-ch19-recall-cp" concept="cp-minus-cv">
  <div slot="front">Why does warming a gas at constant pressure cost more heat than warming it at constant volume?</div>

  At constant pressure the gas expands as it warms and pushes its surroundings back. That work
  leaves as fast as part of the heat arrives. For an ideal gas the extra is $nR$ per kelvin:
  $C_p = C_V + R$.
</Recall>

</Step>

<Step>

## Name it

The gas's internal energy $U$ has two doors: heat $Q$ coming in, and work $W$ the gas does on
its surroundings going out. Count them once, with those signs, and the ledger always balances:

$$\Delta U = Q - W$$

For an ideal gas $U$ tracks the temperature alone, so the thermometer reads the balance.

<Recall id="up-ch19-recall-first-law" concept="first-law-of-thermodynamics">
  <div slot="front">A gas takes in 500 J of heat and pushes a piston out, doing 200 J of work. What happens to its internal energy?</div>

  It rises by 300 J. $\Delta U = Q - W = 500 - 200$. The first law is a ledger for one named
  system: $Q$ is heat into it, $W$ is work done by it.
</Recall>

</Step>

## What to carry forward

- **Heat and work are two doors to one account.** $\Delta U = Q - W$, with $Q$ in and $W$ done by the gas.
- **A gas that can push costs more to warm.** The extra is the work, $nR$ per kelvin: $C_p = C_V + R$.
