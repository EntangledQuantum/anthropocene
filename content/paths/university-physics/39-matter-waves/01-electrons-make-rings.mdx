## Name it

The voltage gives each electron a momentum $p = \sqrt{2m_e eV}$, and the rings behave as if
that momentum carries a wave:

$$\lambda = \frac{h}{p} = \frac{h}{\sqrt{2m_e\,eV}}$$

De Broglie guessed it in 1924 by running the photon's $p = h/\lambda$ backwards. Near light
speed use the relativistic momentum: it moves λ by 0.2 % at 5 kV and 5 % in a 100 kV
electron microscope.

<Recall id="up-ch39-recall-de-broglie" concept="de-broglie-wavelength">
  <div slot="front">A proton and an electron fall through the same 100 V. Whose wavelength is longer, and by how much?</div>

  The electron's, by about 43 times. Both gain the same 100 eV, but $p = \sqrt{2m\,eV}$ and
  the proton is 1836 times heavier: $\sqrt{1836} \approx 43$.
</Recall>

</Step>

## Catch the echo

In 1927 Davisson and Germer fired 54 V electrons straight down onto a nickel crystal, whose
surface atoms sit in rows 0.215 nm apart. Every row sends back an echo, and a detector
hears a strong beam only where the echoes arrive in step.

<CatchTheEcho
  id="up-ch39-catch-the-echo"
  prompt="Swing the detector to where the echoes from every row arrive in step, then fire."
  explanation="About 51°, where each row's echo is exactly one wavelength behind its neighbour's: D·sin φ = λ, chapter 36's grating rule. Davisson and Germer found their peak at 50°. It was the first measurement of a matter wave, and λ = h/p had predicted it."
/>

<Recall id="up-ch39-recall-crystal" concept="electron-diffraction">
  <div slot="front">A ruled grating diffracts light beautifully. Why is it useless for electrons, when a crystal works?</div>

  A grating bends a wave strongly only when its spacing is not much bigger than the
  wavelength. Electrons from tens to thousands of volts have λ between 0.2 and 0.02 nm, the
  spacing of atoms in a crystal. Ruled lines are thousands of times too coarse.
</Recall>

</Step>

<Step>

## Why a baseball never diffracts

Everything with momentum has a wavelength, not only electrons.

<Estimate
  id="up-ch39-baseball-wavelength"
  scenario="up-ch39-baseball-wavelength"
  prompt="A 145 g baseball leaves the pitcher's hand at 40 m/s. What is its de Broglie wavelength?"
  explanation="About 10⁻³⁴ m, ten billion billion times smaller than a proton. Nothing in the universe is a fine enough slit to bend it, so the ball's wave never shows. That, not a different set of laws, is why the everyday world looks like particles."
/>

## What to carry forward

- **Everything with momentum has a wavelength**, λ = h/p. Faster means shorter, and
  heavier means much shorter.
- **A crystal is a grating for electrons.** The angle of the beam measures λ, and λ = h/p
  predicts it.
