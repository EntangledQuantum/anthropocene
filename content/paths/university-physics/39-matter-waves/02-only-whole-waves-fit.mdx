## Name it

A whole number of wavelengths round the ring, $2\pi r = n\lambda$, is chapter 15's
standing wave on a string with its two ends tied together. With the Coulomb pull setting the
speed, it allows only

$$r_n = n^2 a_0,\quad a_0 = 0.0529\ \text{nm},\qquad E_n = -\frac{13.6\ \text{eV}}{n^2}$$

Between those energies there is nothing, so the atom can only change its energy by a jump.

<Recall id="up-ch39-recall-bohr" concept="bohr-model">
  <div slot="front">Why can't hydrogen's electron sit on an orbit of radius 2a₀?</div>

  About 1.41 wavelengths fit round it. The wave cannot join itself, so trip after trip it
  cancels. Only $a_0$, $4a_0$, $9a_0$, … hold a whole number.
</Recall>

</Step>

## Same line, different atom

Helium that has lost one electron, He⁺, is a one-electron atom whose nucleus has twice the
charge. Every level is four times deeper: $E_n = -54.4\ \text{eV}/n^2$.

<Recall id="up-ch39-recall-lines" concept="line-spectra">
  <div slot="front">A gas glows at a few sharp wavelengths instead of a rainbow. What does each line tell you?</div>

  The size of one gap between allowed energies, $\lambda = hc/\Delta E$. Sharp lines mean the
  energies themselves come only in fixed values.
</Recall>

## What to carry forward

- **A bound wave has to fit**, so only a ladder of orbits and energies exists:
  $E_n = -13.6\ \text{eV}/n^2$.
- **Spectral lines are the gaps**: one photon per drop, $\lambda = hc/\Delta E$.
- **Bohr's ring is a costume.** The electron does not travel on a circle; its wave fills
  three dimensions.
