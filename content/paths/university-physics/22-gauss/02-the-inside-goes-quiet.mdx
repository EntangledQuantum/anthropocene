<Step>

## Name it

Gauss's law hands you the field only when symmetry has already made it one value, pointing straight
through the surface. For a tube, that surface is a coaxial sleeve of radius $r$ and length $L$:

$$E \cdot 2\pi r L = \frac{\lambda_{\text{enc}} L}{\varepsilon_0}$$

Outside, $\lambda_{\text{enc}}$ is the tube's whole charge and the tube looks exactly like a rod on
its axis; inside, the sleeve wraps nothing, so $E = 0$.

<Recall id="up-ch22-recall-shell" concept="gaussian-surface">
  <div slot="front">A thin spherical shell carries charge $Q$ spread evenly. Why is the field outside it the same as a point charge $Q$ at its centre?</div>

  A concentric sphere through your position has the same field all over it, pointing straight
  out, so $E \cdot 4\pi r^2 = Q/\varepsilon_0$: the point charge's own equation. The shell's radius
  never enters, and inside it the same sphere wraps nothing.
</Recall>

</Step>

## Crowded at the edge

With no field inside the metal, a sleeve just under the skin wraps no charge, so it all sits on the
surface. A flat sleeve straddling the skin shows the field just outside is $\sigma/\varepsilon_0$,
set by the charge per square metre of surface right there.

  id="up-ch22-strongest-outside"
  prompt="Drag the probe to where the field just outside the bar is strongest."
  lambda={6}
  explanation="Off the sharp edge: 226 N/C there, against 150 off the round end and 110 on the flat flanks. />

<Recall id="up-ch22-recall-conductor" concept="conductor-in-equilibrium">
  <div slot="front">Why must the field inside a piece of metal be zero once its charge has stopped moving?</div>

  Metal is full of charge free to move. Any field inside would push it, so the charge keeps
  moving until it has arranged itself on the surface to cancel every field inside.
</Recall>

## What to carry forward

- **Gauss gives the field only with symmetry**: pick the surface where the field is one value, straight through.
- **Inside a uniform shell, and inside metal at rest, the field is zero.** Extra charge on metal lives on its surface, crowding at sharp edges.
