## Name it

Pressure is the stiffness of the air times how sharply the displacement changes from one
parcel to the next:

$$p = -B\,\frac{\partial s}{\partial x}$$

So the pressure wave and the displacement wave are the same wave, a quarter-wavelength out
of step. The crowding moves at a speed set by the air alone, $v = \sqrt{B/\rho} = 343$ m/s,
and $v = f\lambda$ gives every note its wavelength.

<Recall id="up-ch16-recall-squeeze" concept="sound-wave">
  <div slot="front">At the instant a sound wave has pushed the air at one spot farthest from home, what is the pressure there?</div>

  Normal. The air on either side has moved just as far the same way, so nothing there is
  squeezed or stretched. The pressure peaks a quarter-wavelength away, where the
  displacement passes through zero.
</Recall>

<Step>

## Only odd quarters fit

A tube closed at one end needs still air at the wall and the most motion at the mouth. So it
fits $\tfrac14$, $\tfrac34$, $\tfrac54$ of a wavelength and nothing in between:
$f_n = nv/4L$, $n$ odd. Open at both ends, it needs the most motion at each end, and every
half-wave fits: $f_n = nv/2L$.

<Recall id="up-ch16-recall-closed-pipe" concept="pipe-harmonics">
  <div slot="front">In a pipe closed at one end, where is the pressure node?</div>

  At the open end, which is joined to the room and stays at room pressure. The closed end
  is where the air cannot move, so it is squeezed and stretched hardest there: a
  displacement node is a pressure antinode.
</Recall>

## What to carry forward

- **Sound is air shuffling along its path.** The squeeze sits where the displacement crosses zero, a quarter-wave from where the air moved most.
- **A tube booms when its ends fit the wave**: still air at a wall, moving air at an opening. Closed at one end, only odd quarter-waves fit.
