Two people measure the same gust of wind.

One lays her axes along the road, because that is the direction her car is going. The other
uses north and east, because that is what the weather station reports. They write down four
numbers between them and not one of the four agrees. Neither of them has made a mistake.

So: what did the wind do, and which of their numbers is a fact about it?

Do the two modes back to back. It is the most important minute in this chapter.

**Turn grid**: the components of A swing through their whole range, from 5 down through zero
and out to −5. The length stays 5. The angle between A and B stays 39.1°. The dot product
stays 16, and the little drift readout sits at around $10^{-16}$ — that is floating-point
roundoff, not physics, which is the honest way of saying *exactly unchanged*.

**Turn arrow**: the components do exactly the same thing. But now the dot product moves, the
angle moves, the signed area moves. Something real happened this time.

The left column looks identical in the two modes. Only the right column can tell you which
one was a change of the world and which was a change of your handwriting. That is why the
components are the weakest description of a vector you can carry around.

## Components are two arrows that add up

There is nothing mysterious about where the numbers come from. Pick two perpendicular
arrows of length one — call them $\hat e_1$ and $\hat e_2$ — and every arrow in the plane
is some amount of the first plus some amount of the second. Those two amounts are the
components.

Watch the chain, not the numbers. Two arrows that thrash about, and a sum that is nailed to
the spot. The pair is a *route* to the tip of A, and there are infinitely many routes — one
for every angle of the grid. A is the destination, not the route.

This is also why there is no such thing as the right axes. You place them. On an inclined
plane you will place one along the slope, because it makes a component of gravity vanish and
the algebra collapses. On a circular orbit you will place one along the velocity. Nothing in
nature is holding a set of axes for you.

## What the component does as you turn

You have felt it. Now commit to the shape.

<SketchCurve
  id="up-ch1-component-sketch"
  scenario="up-ch1-component-vs-angle"
  prompt="Take the arrow A from above and turn the grid all the way round, from 0° to 360°. Sketch its first component the whole way. On the square grid it starts at 4."
  hint="There is an angle where the first axis lies exactly along A. What does the component read there — and what does the other component read?"
  explanation="A cosine of amplitude 5, not 4. The curve rises ABOVE its starting value, peaking at exactly the length of A when the first axis lies along it — and at that angle the second component is zero, because the whole arrow has been loaded onto one axis. Ninety degrees later the component passes through zero, and the arrow lies entirely on the other axis. The amplitude is the one thing about this curve that is a fact about A: every grid you could choose gives a component somewhere between −5 and 5."
/>

The peak of that curve is worth naming. There is always an angle at which one component
carries the entire arrow and the other reads zero. A component can be as large as the arrow
— when the axis is lined up with it — and it can never be larger. Both facts are the
amplitude of your cosine.

<Recall id="up-ch1-recall-components" concept="vector-components">
  <div slot="front">A velocity has components $(4, 3)$ on one grid and $(5, 0)$ on another. How many velocities are being described, and how would you tell?</div>

  One. Both readings have magnitude 5, and the second grid is simply the first turned by
  36.87° so that one axis lies along the motion. The components are two different
  *measurements* of a single arrow against two different grids.

  The way to tell is to compute something that does not mention an axis — the magnitude
  here, or the dot product with some other fixed arrow. If those agree, the descriptions
  are of the same object.
</Recall>

## The dot product is a component in a well-chosen frame

Here is the payoff of everything above.

$\vec A\cdot\vec B$ is usually introduced as $A_xB_x + A_yB_y$, which makes it look like a
piece of grid-dependent bookkeeping. It is the opposite of that. Turn the grid so that the
first axis lies along B, and the sum collapses: B has no second component at all, so only
one term survives, and it reads

$$\vec A\cdot\vec B = |\vec B| \times (\text{how much of } \vec A \text{ lies along } \vec B)$$

The dot product is asking one question — *how much of this one points along that one* — and
the component sum is just the answer written in a frame that has not been chosen well.

/>

Two consequences, both of which you will use for the rest of physics.

If A is perpendicular to B, that green segment has zero length and the dot product is zero.
A force at right angles to the motion does **no work**, however hard it pulls — which is why
a satellite in a circular orbit needs no engine, and why the normal force never appears in
an energy budget.

And because the dot product survives every turn of the grid, the work done by a force is a
physical quantity rather than an artefact of how somebody drew the axes. If it were not
invariant, energy conservation would depend on your sketch.

<Recall id="up-ch1-recall-dot" concept="dot-product">
  <div slot="front">Why can a dot product not be undone — that is, why can you not recover $\vec B$ from $\vec A$ and the number $\vec A\cdot\vec B$?</div>

  Because it throws information away. It returns a single number where two arrows went in,
  keeping only how much of one lies along the other and discarding everything perpendicular.

  Every arrow whose tip sits anywhere on a particular line perpendicular to $\vec A$ gives
  the same dot product with $\vec A$. There are infinitely many of them, so there is nothing
  to divide back out — which is also why "the dot product is how you multiply vectors" is a
  misleading thing to carry around.
</Recall>

## What to carry forward

- An arrow is **what it is**; its components are what one grid says about it. Turn the grid
  and every number changes while nothing moves.
- **Nothing in nature holds the axes.** Choosing them along a slope, along a force, or along
  the motion is free, and a good choice makes a component vanish.
- The things that **survive a turn** — length, angle, dot product, spanned area — are the
  physical statements. When you want to know whether two descriptions are of the same
  situation, compute one of those.
- The dot product is **how much of this lies along that**, and it is a single component in
  the frame you would have chosen anyway.
