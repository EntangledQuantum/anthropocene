## Name it

The heat that melts ice changes its phase, not its temperature. Per kilogram it is the
**latent heat of fusion**, and it enters the budget before any warming does:

$$Q = mL_f, \qquad L_f = 334\ \text{kJ/kg}$$

<Recall id="up-ch17-recall-iced-drink" concept="latent-heat">
  <div slot="front">A drink with ice still in it sits at 0 °C for a long time, even in a warm room. Why doesn't it warm up?</div>

  Every joule leaking in goes into melting ice instead of warming the drink. The
  thermometer cannot move until the ice is gone, and that costs 334 kJ per kilogram of ice.
</Recall>

## Keep the ice until evening

A cool box holding 3 kg of ice spends a 12-hour day in 30 °C shade, and you need 1 kg left
at the end. Foam takes up room, so you want the thinnest wall that does the job.

<CoolBoxDay
  id="up-ch17-thinnest-wall"
  prompt="Set the wall thickness, run the day, and check once it is over."
  L0={2}
  explanation="Just over 3.2 cm. You can afford to melt 2 kg, which is 668 kJ, over 43,200 s: a leak of 15.5 W. Heat conducts through a slab at P = kAΔT/L, so L = 0.033 × 0.5 × 30 / 15.5 = 3.2 cm. The inside never left 0 °C; the ice paid for every joule that got through."
/>

<Recall id="up-ch17-recall-double-wall" concept="thermal-conduction">
  <div slot="front">You double the thickness of a cool box's foam. What happens to the heat leaking in, and to the temperature inside while there is ice?</div>

  The leak halves, because $P = kA\,\Delta T/L$. The inside stays at 0 °C either way: the
  thicker wall only makes the ice last twice as long.
</Recall>

## What to carry forward

- **While a phase changes, heat goes in and the thermometer holds still.** Melting ice costs 334 kJ per kilogram.
- **Heat conducts at $P = kA\,\Delta T/L$.** What a surface feels like, and what insulation does, are both about that rate. Nothing carries "cold".
