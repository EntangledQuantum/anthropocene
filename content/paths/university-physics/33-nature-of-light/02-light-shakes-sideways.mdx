## Name it

Light's electric field points across the beam, and its direction is the light's **polarisation**. A filter passes the part of the field along its slots, and intensity goes as the field squared, which is **Malus's law**:

$$I = I_0 \cos^2\theta$$

<Recall id="up-ch33-recall-malus" concept="malus-law">
  <div slot="front">Polarised light meets a filter turned 60° from its field. What fraction gets through, and why that and not one half?</div>

  A quarter. The filter passes the part of the field along its axis, $\cos 60° = \tfrac12$
  of it, and intensity goes as the square of the field.
</Recall>

</Step>

## Turn it without losing it

One filter at 45° turns the light a right angle and keeps a quarter of it. Your target is four-fifths.

<TwistWithFilters

  explanation="Twelve filters, each turned 7.5°, keep 81%; eleven keep 79.9%. Each small turn costs cos² of a small angle, almost nothing, and the losses shrink faster than the filters multiply. />

<Recall id="up-ch33-recall-third" concept="polarization">
  <div slot="front">Why does a third filter at 45° let light through two crossed filters?</div>

  A filter does not only remove light: it sets the direction of the field it passes. The 45°
  filter leaves the field at 45°, which has a part along the last filter's axis. One-eighth
  of the lamp's light gets through.
</Recall>

## What to carry forward

- **Light's field points across the beam.** A filter passes the part along its axis, $\cos\theta$ of the field and $\cos^2\theta$ of the light.
- **A filter also sets the direction.** Many small turns can rotate light almost for free.
- **Reflections are polarised along the surface**, completely at Brewster's angle, $\tan\theta_B = n_2/n_1$.
