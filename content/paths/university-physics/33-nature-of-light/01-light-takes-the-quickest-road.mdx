## Name it

Light does what the lifeguard did. It crosses from one point to another by the **quickest** route, which is Fermat's principle. Each material slows it by a factor $n$, its **index of refraction**, and the quickest route across a boundary obeys **Snell's law**:

$$n = \frac{c}{v}, \qquad n_1 \sin\theta_1 = n_2 \sin\theta_2$$

<Recall id="up-ch33-recall-quickest" concept="fermats-principle">
  <div slot="front">Light goes from a point in air to a point under water. Why is its path not a straight line?</div>

  It takes the quickest route, not the shortest. Light is 1.33 times slower in water, so the
  quickest path runs further through the air and meets the surface more steeply below:
  $\sin\theta_{\text{air}} = 1.33\,\sin\theta_{\text{water}}$.
</Recall>

</Step>

## Trap the light

Now run it backwards: light inside glass, trying to get out into faster air. Leaving, it bends away from the normal.

<TrapTheBeam

  explanation="41.8°, where sin θ = 1/1.5. Beyond it the escaping ray would need a sine bigger than 1, so there is none, and every bit of light reflects. />

<Recall id="up-ch33-recall-critical" concept="total-internal-reflection">
  <div slot="front">Can light going from air into water ever be totally reflected?</div>

  No. Going into a slower material the ray bends toward the normal, and there is always a
  refracted ray. Only light trying to leave the slower material can be trapped, beyond
  $\sin\theta_c = n_2/n_1$.
</Recall>

## What to carry forward

- **Light takes the quickest road.** Where it crosses into a material $n$ times slower, $n_1\sin\theta_1 = n_2\sin\theta_2$.
- **The frequency never changes at a boundary;** the wavelength shrinks by $n$.
- **Leaving a slow material beyond the critical angle, nothing gets out.**
