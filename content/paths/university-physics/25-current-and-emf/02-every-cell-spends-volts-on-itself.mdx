## Name it

The pump inside the cell does 1.5 J of work on every coulomb it lifts: that is its **emf**, $\mathcal E$, in joules per coulomb, or volts. It is not a force. Some of that energy is spent pushing the current through the cell's own resistance $r$, and the terminals get the rest:

$$V_{\text{terminal}} = \mathcal E - I r$$

<Recall id="up-ch25-recall-label-voltage" concept="emf">
  <div slot="front">A battery is labelled 9 V. When does a voltmeter across its terminals read exactly 9 V?</div>

  Only when it delivers no current. The label is the emf, the work per coulomb its chemistry
  does. Draw a current $I$ and $Ir$ of it is spent inside, so the terminals read less.
</Recall>

</Step>

## Make it as bright as it goes

The same worn cell drives one lamp, and you choose the lamp's resistance.

<MatchTheLoad
  id="up-ch25-brightest-lamp"
  prompt="Slide the contact until the lamp takes the most power it can get, then check."
  explanation="0.5 Ω, the cell's own resistance, for 1.125 W. Less resistance draws more current, but more of the emf is lost inside and the lamp's voltage falls faster than its current rises. At the peak the cell heats itself exactly as much as it lights the lamp: half the energy stays behind."
/>

<Recall id="up-ch25-recall-used-up" concept="electrical-power">
  <div slot="front">A cell lights a lamp. Of current, charge and energy, which does the lamp use up?</div>

  Only energy. The same current enters and leaves the lamp, and no charge is lost. Each
  coulomb gives up energy as it passes, at a rate $P = IV = I^2R = V^2/R$.
</Recall>

## Power or efficiency

At $R = r$ the load gets the most power and the source wastes as much again. Sometimes that is the right trade.

## What to carry forward

- **A real cell is a pump plus a resistor.** Its terminals read $\mathcal E - Ir$, sagging along a straight line to zero at $\mathcal E/r$.
- **A load takes the most power at $R = r$**, and then the source wastes as much as it delivers.
- **A lamp uses up energy, not current.** $P = IV = I^2R = V^2/R$.
