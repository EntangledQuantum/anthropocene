<Step>

## Name it

The dashed iris segment was the **lever arm** $r_\perp$: the distance from the hinge to the
push's line. Force times lever arm is the **torque**, and a net torque gives an angular
acceleration the way a net force gives a linear one:

$$\tau = r_\perp F = rF\sin\varphi, \qquad \tau_{\text{net}} = I\alpha$$

<Recall id="up-ch10-recall-lever-arm" concept="torque">
  <div slot="front">Can a 500 N push on a door turn it not at all?</div>

  Yes, if its line of action passes through the hinge: a push at the hinge itself, or one
  straight along the door. The lever arm is zero, so the torque is zero, however hard you
  push.
</Recall>

</Step>

<Recall id="up-ch10-recall-heavy-door" concept="rotational-second-law">
  <div slot="front">Why does a vault door open under one hand, but slowly?</div>

  Any net torque gives $\alpha = \tau/I$. A huge $I$ makes $\alpha$ small, never zero, and a
  small $\alpha$ still builds up speed over time.
</Recall>

## What to carry forward

- **A twist is force times lever arm.** The lever arm runs from the axis to the push's line, not to the push's point.
- **Torques have a sense.** Opposite twists cancel even while the forces are all still there.

Next: spin something with no torque at all, and watch it speed up anyway.
