## Load the coin

Now run the box in ticks. On every tick each live nucleus flips the same coin, and if it lands one way, that nucleus decays.

<Step>

## Name it

A fixed chance per unit time, $\lambda$, for every nucleus, is the whole law:

$$N = N_0\,e^{-\lambda t} = N_0\left(\tfrac12\right)^{t/t_{1/2}}, \qquad t_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{\lambda}$$

The half-life is not a lifetime. It is how long the same coin takes to halve whatever is there.

<Recall id="up-ch43-recall-overdue" concept="radioactive-decay-law">
  <div slot="front">A nucleus has survived five half-lives. What is its chance of decaying during the next one?</div>

  50%, the same as a nucleus made a moment ago. Nothing inside it counts time, so no nucleus is
  ever overdue.
</Recall>

</Step>

<Recall id="up-ch43-recall-two-half-lives" concept="half-life">
  <div slot="front">What fraction of a radioactive sample is left after two half-lives? After ten?</div>

  A quarter, then about a thousandth ($2^{-10}$). Each half-life halves what is there, so the
  sample thins out forever instead of running out on schedule.
</Recall>

## What to carry forward

- **Each nucleus is a coin.** The same chance every second, whatever its age, and nothing says which goes next. In alpha decay the coin is chapter 40's tunnelling: the alpha particle hits the wall again and again with the same tiny chance of getting through.
- **The count halves on a schedule.** Two half-lives leave a quarter, and the half-life is the same from any starting point.
