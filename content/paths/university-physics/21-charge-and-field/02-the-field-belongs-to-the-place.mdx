## Name it

Every bead read the same thing off the spot: 0.80 mN per nC, which is $8.0\times10^{5}$ N/C.
That is the **electric field**, and a point charge sets it at every place around itself:

$$\vec E = \dfrac{\vec F}{q}, \qquad E = k\,\dfrac{|Q|}{r^{2}}$$

<Recall id="up-ch21-recall-field" concept="electric-field">
  <div slot="front">At some point the field is 500 N/C pointing east. What is the force on an electron there?</div>

  $8.0\times10^{-17}$ N pointing west. $\vec F = q\vec E$ with $q = -1.6\times10^{-19}$ C: the
  size is $qE$, and the minus sign turns it around.
</Recall>

</Step>

<Recall id="up-ch21-recall-dipole" concept="electric-dipole">
  <div slot="front">A water molecule is neutral. Why does it still attract charged things?</div>

  It is a dipole: its + and − charge sit in slightly different places, so their fields do not
  quite cancel. The leftover field is strong close by and falls as $1/r^3$.
</Recall>

## What to carry forward

- **The field is force per charge, and it belongs to the place.** $\vec F = q\vec E$ for any charge you put there.
- **Fields add as arrows at every point**, and where they cancel the map goes dark.
- **Neutral is not fieldless.** A dipole's field falls as $1/r^3$.
