## Every ring pushes back

Freeze the copper pipe mid-fall and cut it open. The magnet, north pole down, sinks between two
rings of copper.

<Step>

## Name it

The moving bar sweeps out area, so the flux grows at $BLv$. The current feels a force $ILB$, and
Lenz fixes its direction: always against the motion.

$$\mathcal E = BLv, \qquad F_{\text{drag}} = ILB = \frac{B^2L^2v}{R}, \qquad F_{\text{drag}}\,v = \frac{\mathcal E^2}{R}$$

<Recall id="up-ch29-recall-lenz" concept="lenz-law">
  <div slot="front">Suppose the induced current pushed the bar along instead of holding it back. What would one small nudge do?</div>

  Run away. The bar would speed up, drive more current, get pushed harder, and light the bulb
  from nothing. The minus sign in Faraday's law is energy conservation.
</Recall>

</Step>

<Recall id="up-ch29-recall-eddy" concept="eddy-currents">
  <div slot="front">Why does a slot down the copper pipe let the magnet fall almost freely?</div>

  The braking currents run round the pipe. A slot cuts every one of those loops, so almost no
  current flows and almost nothing pushes back.
</Recall>

## What to carry forward

- **An induced current always opposes the change that drives it.** Ahead of a magnet it repels; behind, it pulls back.
- **Every induced watt is paid for** by whatever makes the change: your hand, or a falling magnet's weight.
