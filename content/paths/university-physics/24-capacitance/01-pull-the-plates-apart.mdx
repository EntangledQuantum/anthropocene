<Step>

## Name it

At any gap, charge and voltage kept a fixed ratio. That ratio is the **capacitance**, and for two flat plates it is set by geometry alone:

$$Q = CV, \qquad C = \dfrac{\varepsilon_0 A}{d}$$

These plates are 44 pF at 2 mm. Close the gap and $C$ grows; the battery then fixes $V$, or the cut wire fixes $Q$, and the other follows.

<Recall id="up-ch24-recall-q-or-v" concept="capacitance">
  <div slot="front">A charged capacitor has its gap halved. What happens to Q and V, with the battery connected and with it disconnected?</div>

  Connected: V stays, C doubles, so Q doubles (the battery supplies it). Disconnected: Q
  stays, C doubles, so V halves. The capacitor itself stays neutral throughout: +Q on one
  plate, −Q on the other.
</Recall>

</Step>

## Where the energy goes

The plates attract, so pulling them apart takes work, and a charged capacitor stores energy
$U = \tfrac12 QV$. With the battery on, you pull the plates from 1 mm to 3 mm: you do work, and the field loses energy.

<Recall id="up-ch24-recall-energy" concept="capacitor-energy">
  <div slot="front">With the battery disconnected, you pull the plates of a charged capacitor apart. Where does the work you do go?</div>

  Into the field. The charge is fixed, the attraction ½QE never weakens, and the stored energy
  $Q^2/2C$ rises by exactly your work. Connected, the voltage is fixed instead and the energy
  falls, so your work and the field's loss both go back into the battery.
</Recall>

## What to carry forward

- **Once C is set, Q and V are locked together.** A connected battery fixes V; an open switch fixes Q.
- **C = ε₀A/d is geometry.** Close the gap and the same voltage holds more charge.
- **Pulling apart costs work**, stored in the field if Q is fixed, returned to the battery if V is.

Next: put something in the gap, and C grows without moving the plates.
