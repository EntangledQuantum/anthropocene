## Two jobs, one arrow

Every acceleration does two things at once, and they are cleanly separable:

- the part **along** the velocity, $a_t$, changes the **speed** — nothing else can
- the part **across** it, $a_n$, changes the **direction** — nothing else can

That is the whole of this chapter. The angle between the two arrows decides the split, and
$a_t = |\vec a|\cos\theta$ falls straight out of it.

So there should be an angle at which the speed stops changing altogether. Find it.

Now hold the arrow perpendicular yourself, and keep it there. In this world the arrow is
**glued to the velocity**, so it turns as you turn — which is what steering is. A car's
wheels point relative to the car, not relative to north.

You have just built **uniform circular motion**, and you built it by *removing* something.
Constant speed on a circle is not a special law. It is the case where the acceleration is
pure steering — always perpendicular, so never changing the speed, and always pointing at
the one place a perpendicular can point: the centre.

The words are worth separating. The **speed** is constant. The **velocity** is not — it is
turning through a full circle every lap, and that turning is the acceleration.

<Recall id="up-ch3-recall-split" concept="tangential-normal-acceleration">
  <div slot="front">A car goes round a bend at a perfectly steady 50 km/h. Is it accelerating, and if so, in which direction?</div>

  Yes, and toward the inside of the bend. The speedometer never moves, so $a_t = 0$ — but
  the velocity's *direction* is changing continuously, which is the perpendicular part
  $a_n$. An acceleration perpendicular to the velocity is exactly the one that changes
  direction while leaving the speed alone.
</Recall>

## How much steering does a bend demand?

The circle you were riding had a radius, and it was not a free parameter: hold a speed $v$
with a perpendicular acceleration $a_n$ and the path has to bend on a circle of radius
$R = v^2/a_n$. Read it the other way and you have the formula that shows up everywhere from
a motorway curve to an electron in a magnetic field:

$$a_n = \frac{v^2}{R}$$

The $v^2$ is the part that catches people out.

<Recall id="up-ch3-recall-centripetal" concept="centripetal-acceleration">
  <div slot="front">You take the same bend at twice the speed. By what factor does the acceleration the road must supply go up — and why is "centripetal force" not an extra arrow on the free-body diagram?</div>

  Four times, because $a_n = v^2/R$ and only $v$ is squared. And "centripetal" names a
  *direction*, not a source: the inward pull is supplied by something real — friction from
  the road, tension in a string, gravity for an orbit. Drawing it as an additional arrow
  beside those counts the same force twice.
</Recall>

## What to carry forward

- **One arrow, two jobs.** Project the acceleration onto the velocity and you get the speed
  change; what is left over is the steering. Every question in this chapter is one of those
  two components.
- **Perpendicular means pure turning.** Uniform circular motion is the case with the
  tangential part deleted, and $a_n = v^2/R$ is what "how hard" looks like as a number.
- The picture to keep: **a velocity arrow whose tip is being dragged sideways.** It is the
  same picture for a planet, a proton in a cyclotron and a car on a roundabout.

Next: pin the arrow to the world instead of to the velocity, point it straight down, and the
same machinery gives you a projectile — two one-dimensional motions reading one clock.
