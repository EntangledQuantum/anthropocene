Take the arrow from the last lesson and stop steering with it. Pin it to the world, point it
straight down, and never touch it again. That is gravity, and everything a projectile does
follows from an arrow that never moves.

Here is the oldest trap in the subject. Two identical balls leave the edge of a table at the
same instant. One is flicked sideways. One is simply let go.

## One clock, two independent stories

The two panels under the flight are the whole idea. The horizontal story is
$x = v_{0x}t$: nothing accelerates it, so the graph is straight and the strobe marks are
evenly spaced sideways forever. The vertical story is $y = v_{0y}t - \tfrac12 g t^2$: the
free fall you already know, unaffected by anything happening sideways.

They share exactly one thing, and it is $t$.

Launch at an angle and the companion ball becomes a ball thrown **straight up** with the
same vertical speed. Its height matches the launched ball's at every instant — that is the
thick band lying underneath the $y(t)$ curve.

That last option pair is lesson one in new clothes. At the apex the acceleration is
perpendicular to the velocity, so $a_t = 0$: for one instant the projectile is doing exactly
what the ball on the circle was doing — pure steering.

  ball gets during the time the falling already decided.

## Now put the air back

Everything above assumed nothing but gravity. Real air pushes back, and how hard depends on
how fast — for a ball at everyday speeds, on the *square* of how fast. That gives every
object a speed at which the push balances its weight.

Commit to a number before you see it.

## The thing that quietly broke

Drag does not just shorten the flight. Turn it on in the panel above and look at the level
rule between the launched ball and the companion.

<Recall id="up-ch3-recall-drag" concept="terminal-velocity-preview">
  <div slot="front">Name two things that are true of a drag-free projectile and false once air resistance is switched on.</div>

  Any two of: the climb and the descent take the same time; it lands at the speed and angle
  it left at; the horizontal velocity is constant; 45° gives the greatest range; the
  horizontal and vertical motions are independent. All five come from the same source — a
  drag force that points along the velocity and scales with the total speed, which breaks
  both the symmetry in time and the separation of the two stories.
</Recall>

## What to carry forward

- A projectile is **two one-dimensional problems sharing a stopwatch**. Solve the vertical
  one for the timing, then hand that time to the horizontal one for the distance.
- **Nothing horizontal ever happens** without air: constant sideways velocity, straight
  $x(t)$, evenly spaced strobes, and a landing time that does not care how hard you threw.
- The picture to keep: **two balls falling level with each other**, one of them also
  travelling sideways at a steady rate.
- And the honest edge: the parabola is a model with a speed limit. Compare the flight to
  the object's terminal speed before you trust it.
