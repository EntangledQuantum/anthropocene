## One curve, read three ways

Drag the handles. The velocity curve is yours; position and acceleration are computed from
it and cannot be edited, because there is nothing there to edit. Position is the area that
has piled up underneath. Acceleration is the slope.

<LinkedGraphs
  edit="v"
  knots={[[0, 0], [2.5, 5], [5, 5], [7.5, -2], [10, 0]]}
  duration={10}
  caption="Make a bump and watch the acceleration panel go positive then negative. Make a flat stretch and watch it die to zero. You are not drawing three graphs — you are drawing one and reading it three ways."
/>

Two things are worth doing deliberately before you move on.

Flatten the curve somewhere in the middle. The acceleration goes to zero there while the
velocity is still large — **moving fast and accelerating are unrelated questions.** Then
drag a handle below the axis and watch the position panel turn downward. Area below the
line subtracts.

## The thing a velocity graph cannot tell you

Everything above came out of one curve. Almost.

Notice that the position panel has to start *somewhere*, and nothing in the velocity
history says where. Slide the whole position curve up by fifty metres and every slope on
it is unchanged — so it is still a perfectly good answer to the same velocity graph. The
area gives you the **change** in position. Somebody has to hand you the starting point
separately.

That missing constant is not a technicality. It is the reason `requires` an initial
condition appears in every differential equation you will meet for the rest of this path.

<Recall id="up-recall-constant" concept="accumulation">
  <div slot="front">You are given $v(t)$ over an interval and asked for $x(t)$. What is missing, and why?</div>

  The starting position. Integrating $v$ gives the *change* in position, $\Delta x$, and
  every curve shifted vertically has identical slopes everywhere — so infinitely many
  $x(t)$ are consistent with one $v(t)$. The constant of integration is exactly the piece
  of information the velocity history never contained.
</Recall>

## Two different answers to "how far"

The sketch you just drew ends around twelve metres from the origin. The odometer says
something else entirely.

<Estimate
  id="up-estimate-distance"
  scenario="up-distance-vs-displacement"
  prompt="Over those ten seconds, how much ground did the object actually cover?"
  explanation="About 40 m of travel, ending 12 m from home. Displacement adds the signed area; distance adds the magnitudes, so the return trip counts twice over rather than cancelling. Both numbers are true about the same run — they answer different questions."
/>

Displacement is the directed gap between start and finish. Distance is what the odometer
turned. They agree only when the motion never reverses, which is why the distinction is
invisible until something turns around — and then it is worth eight marks.

<Recall id="up-recall-distance" concept="distance-vs-displacement">
  <div slot="front">A runner completes one full lap of a 400 m track. What is the distance travelled, and what is the displacement?</div>

  400 m of distance, and zero displacement. Both statements describe the same lap
  correctly. Distance accumulates $|v|$ and never decreases; displacement accumulates the
  signed $v$ and returns to zero because the runner came back to where they started.
</Recall>

## Shape a motion to order

One more, and this one you have to hit rather than recognise.

/>

There is no formula for this. There is a shape — go out, come back, and make the two areas
match — and once you have felt it, "the net area is the displacement" stops being a
sentence you memorised.

## What to carry forward

- There is **one curve**. Velocity is its slope, acceleration is the slope of that slope,
  and the three panels can never disagree because there is nothing to disagree about.
- **Area accumulates, and it is signed.** Below the axis subtracts. That single idea is
  the integral, and it will come back as work, as impulse, as charge, as flux.
- A rate history gives you the **change**, never the value. The starting point arrives
  separately, every time.
