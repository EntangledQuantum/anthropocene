## Name it

Current flows only while voltage is left across the resistor, and that leftover shrinks as the
capacitor fills. So every stretch of $RC$ seconds closes the same fraction of what is left:

$$V_C = \mathcal{E}\left(1 - e^{-t/RC}\right), \qquad \tau = RC$$

One time constant brings it to 63 %, and three bring it to 95 %. Ohms times farads is seconds.

<Recall id="up-ch26-recall-tau" concept="rc-time-constant">
  <div slot="front">A capacitor charges through 2 kΩ. How long does a 500 µF capacitor take to reach 63 % of the supply voltage?</div>

  One time constant: $RC = 2000 \times 500\times10^{-6} = 1$ s. The 63 % is $1 - e^{-1}$, and it
  holds for every R and C.
</Recall>

</Step>

<Recall id="up-ch26-recall-rc-energy" concept="rc-circuit">
  <div slot="front">A capacitor discharges until its voltage has halved. What fraction of its energy is left?</div>

  A quarter. Energy is $\tfrac12 CV^2$, so it goes as the square of the voltage. Half the energy is
  left at $V_0/\sqrt2$, about 71 % of the voltage.
</Recall>

## What to carry forward

- **Quickly, then slowly.** A capacitor charges through a resistor on a clock set by $\tau = RC$: 63 % at $\tau$, 95 % at $3\tau$.
- **Energy goes as $V^2$.** Charging through a resistor always costs as much heat as it stores, and a discharge sends all of it into the resistor.

Next: charges in motion feel a second force, and it comes from other moving charges.
