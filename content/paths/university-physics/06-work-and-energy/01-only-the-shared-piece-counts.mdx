## Name the piece you moved

The **work** of a constant force is its signed parallel component times displacement:

$$W = F_{\parallel}d = Fd\cos\theta = \vec F\cdot\Delta\vec r.$$

One newton acting along one metre contributes one **joule**, $1\,\mathrm J=1\,\mathrm{N\,m}$.
The rectangle's height is $F_{\parallel}$, its width is $d$, and its signed area is $W$.
A perpendicular force has zero height, however long its arrow.

<Recall id="up-ch6-recall-shared-piece" concept="mechanical-work">
  <div slot="front">A force is large but its work is zero. What two pictures could explain that?</div>

  No displacement at all, or a force perpendicular to the displacement. In either picture
  there is no shared piece to accumulate. Mechanical work is not a measurement of how tired
  the person applying the force feels.
</Recall>

## A positive entry is not the total

Now the block is already moving right. Keep the 60 N pull horizontal and add a backward
brake. The fixed track prevents vertical motion; its transverse reaction and gravity do
zero work.

The motion-energy is **kinetic energy**, $K=\tfrac12mv^2$. It depends on speed, not direction.
For this particle, adding every force's signed work gives the change in that energy:

$$W_{\mathrm{net}} = \sum_i W_i = K_f-K_i.$$

That is the **work–energy theorem**: another accounting of Newton's law, not an extra force.
At a 60 N brake, the two entries are +240 J and −240 J. Their sum is zero while neither
entry is zero.

<Recall id="up-ch6-recall-net-account" concept="work-energy-theorem">
  <div slot="front">A pull does +100 J of work, but the particle finishes at its original speed. What can you infer about the other forces?</div>

  Together they do −100 J. Equal starting and ending speed means $\Delta K=0$, so the
  signed work contributions must sum to zero. You cannot infer that each force does no work.
</Recall>

## What to carry forward

Keep the two arrows: **only their shared piece counts, with its sign**.
Keep the whole account: **net** work changes kinetic energy; one force's work is only one entry.
Next, let the force change along the path — the rectangle becomes a signed area under a curve.
