<Step>

## Name it

Only the solid arrow ever reached the speedometer: the piece of the pull along the motion,
times the distance. That product is the **work** the rope does.

$$W = F_\parallel\, d = F d\cos\theta = \vec F\cdot\vec d$$

Past 90° the along-the-ice piece points backward, and the work is negative: the rope takes
energy out.

<Recall id="up-ch06-recall-shared-piece" concept="mechanical-work">
  <div slot="front">A rope pulls 60 N straight up on a sled sliding over flat ice. How much work does it do over 8 m?</div>

  None. Work counts only the piece of a force along the displacement, and straight up has no
  piece along flat ice, however hard it pulls.
</Recall>

</Step>

## Add up every force

The brakes did negative work equal to the whole ½mv² the car had. Every force does its own
work with its own sign, and their sum is the change in kinetic energy:

$$W_{\text{net}} = \sum_i \vec F_i\cdot\vec d = \Delta K,\qquad K = \tfrac12 mv^2$$

Now the sled is on snow, which drags harder the harder the runners press down.

<Recall id="up-ch06-recall-net-work" concept="work-energy-theorem">
  <div slot="front">You push a crate across a rough floor at a steady speed. Who does work on it, and how much in total?</div>

  You do positive work and friction does the same amount of negative work. The total is zero,
  which is why the kinetic energy does not change. A zero sum does not mean zero terms.

## What to carry forward

- **Only the shared piece counts.** Work is the part of a force along the motion, times the
  distance: $W = \vec F\cdot\vec d$.
- **Every force keeps its own account.** Add them with their signs and you get the change
  in ½mv².

Next: when the force changes along the way, the work becomes an area.
