<Step>

## Name it

The wave obeys the Schrödinger equation: it bends back toward zero, and bends harder the more kinetic energy $E - U$ it has. Pinned to zero at both walls, it fits only when whole half-wavelengths span the box, $L = n\lambda/2$, and de Broglie's $\lambda = h/p$ turns that into a ladder of energies:

$$-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\psi'' + U\psi = E\psi \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad E_n = \frac{n^2h^2}{8mL^2}$$

It is chapter 15's rope again: still at both ends, so only certain notes.

<Recall id="up-ch40-recall-floor" concept="particle-in-a-box">
  <div slot="front">Why can an electron in a box never have zero energy?</div>

  Its wave must be zero at both walls, so at least half a wavelength has to span the box. A
  wavelength that short means momentum $h/\lambda$, and so kinetic energy: $h^2/8mL^2$.
</Recall>

</Step>

The rule you used: the chance of finding it between $x$ and $x + dx$ is $|\psi|^2\,dx$, and all the chances add to one, $\int|\psi|^2\,dx = 1$.

<Recall id="up-ch40-recall-born" concept="born-rule">
  <div slot="front">A detector sits under a trough of ψ, where ψ is negative. Does it click less than one under a crest of the same size?</div>

  No. Clicks go as ψ², and a trough squared is as big as a crest. Only a node, where ψ is zero,
  never clicks.
</Recall>

## What to carry forward

- **A trapped wave fits only at certain energies.** Zero at both walls means whole half-wavelengths, $E_n = n^2h^2/8mL^2$, and the lowest is never zero.
- **ψ² is where you find it.** A trough counts as much as a crest; a node never clicks.
