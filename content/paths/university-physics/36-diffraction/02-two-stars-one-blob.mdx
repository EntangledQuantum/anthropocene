## One on the other's dark ring

Each star alone is a bright disc circled by faint rings; the dashed circle is star A's first
dark ring. Astronomers call a pair split when one star sits on the other's first dark ring.

## Name it

A round opening of diameter $D$ blurs every point into a disc whose first dark ring sits at

$$\sin\theta = 1.22\,\frac{\lambda}{D}$$

That angle is the finest detail the opening can show. The 1.22 comes from the round shape; a
slit's first dark is at exactly $\lambda/a$.

<Recall id="up-ch36-recall-headlights" concept="rayleigh-criterion">
  <div slot="front">Your pupil is about 3 mm wide at night. How far away can a car be and still show its two headlights, 1.5 m apart, as two?</div>

  About 7 km. Your eye's limit is $1.22 \times 550\ \text{nm} / 3\ \text{mm} = 2.2\times10^{-4}$
  rad, and 1.5 m subtends that at 6.7 km. Farther away, the lamps merge into one, however
  sharp your eyesight.
</Recall>

## From two slits to hundreds

Two slits paint broad stripes, evenly spaced. A grating is the same thing with hundreds of
slits at the same spacing.

## Split the yellow

A mercury lamp's yellow is two colours, 577.0 nm and 579.1 nm, and the eyepiece looks at the
first-order line. The rule is the stars' rule: the pair is split when one line's peak sits on
the other's first dark.

<Recall id="up-ch36-recall-grating" concept="diffraction-grating">
  <div slot="front">A grating will not split sodium’s two yellow lines, 589.0 nm and 589.6 nm, in first order. Name two ways to split them.</div>

  Uncover more slits: about 980 in first order, since $\lambda/\Delta\lambda \approx 980$. Or
  look in second order, where the lines are twice as far apart and half as many slits do,
  because the rule is $Nm = \lambda/\Delta\lambda$. A stronger eyepiece does neither.
</Recall>

## What to carry forward

- **Sharpness is bought with width.** A round opening blurs each point to $1.22\lambda/D$; a grating's lines narrow as $1/N$.
- **Just split** means one peak on the other's first dark.
- **Magnifying never splits a blur.** It enlarges the blur and the gap alike.
