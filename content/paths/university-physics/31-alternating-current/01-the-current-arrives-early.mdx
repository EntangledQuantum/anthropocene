<Step>

## Name it

A sine wave is the shadow of a turning arrow, a **phasor**: its length is the peak and its
angle is the moment. A capacitor's current is how fast its charge $q = Cv$ changes, so its
arrow turns a quarter turn ahead of the voltage's:

$$i = C\,\frac{dv}{dt} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \text{current leads by } 90^\circ$$

<Recall id="up-ch31-recall-phasor" concept="phasor">
  <div slot="front">A phasor points straight left. What is its sine wave doing at that instant?</div>

  Passing through zero on its way down. The shadow is the arrow's height, which is zero when
  it points sideways; turning anticlockwise from straight left, the tip swings downward.
</Recall>

</Step>

## The coil does the opposite

Swap the capacitor for a coil. A coil's voltage is $L\,di/dt$: it pushes hardest when its
current is changing fastest.

/>

<Recall id="up-ch31-recall-lead-lag" concept="current-voltage-phase">
  <div slot="front">On AC, whose current peaks before its voltage: a capacitor's or a coil's?</div>

  The capacitor's, by a quarter cycle: its current is how fast its charge changes, $C\,dv/dt$.
  A coil's current peaks a quarter cycle after its voltage, because the voltage sets how fast
  that current changes.
</Recall>

## What the socket's number means

The mains arrow is 325 V long. A heater does not care which way the current goes, only how
fast it is being heated.

<MatchTheGlow
  id="up-ch31-match-the-glow"

  explanation="{v} V, the number printed on every European socket. The mains heater's power pulses from 0 to 1058 W twice a cycle and averages exactly half its peak, 529 W. Half the power takes 1/√2 of the voltage: 325 V ÷ √2 = 230 V."

## Name it, and a catch

Mains is labelled with its **RMS** voltage, the steady voltage that heats a resistor just as
fast: $V_{\text{rms}} = V_0/\sqrt2$. Ammeters on AC read RMS amps the same way.

<Recall id="up-ch31-recall-rms" concept="rms">
  <div slot="front">US mains is labelled 120 V. What is its peak voltage?</div>

  About 170 V. The label is the RMS value, and for a sine the peak is √2 times larger:
  $120 \times 1.414 \approx 170$ V.
</Recall>

## What to carry forward

- **A sine is a turning arrow.** Its angle is the moment; adding sines is adding arrows.
- **A capacitor's current leads by a quarter turn; a coil's lags by one.** Neither takes
  any average power.
- **230 V is RMS**: the steady voltage that heats the same. The peak is 325 V.
