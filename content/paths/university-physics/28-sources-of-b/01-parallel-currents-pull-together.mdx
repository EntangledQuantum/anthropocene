## Name it

Grip the wire with your right hand, thumb along the current, and your fingers curl the way
the field runs. Each short piece of current adds a field that circles it and falls as $1/r^2$,
the **Biot–Savart law**; add the pieces of an endless line and the field falls as $1/r$:

$$d\vec B = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\,\frac{I\,d\vec\ell\times\hat r}{r^2}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad B = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r},\quad \mu_0 = 4\pi\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm{T\,m/A}$$

<Recall id="up-ch28-recall-wire" concept="wire-magnetic-field">
  <div slot="front">A long wire carries current straight up out of the floor. Which way does a compass beside it point, and how strong is the field 20 cm away compared with 10 cm?</div>

  Round the wire, counterclockwise seen from above, and at right angles to the line to the
  wire. At 20 cm it is half as strong: a line's field falls as $1/r$.
</Recall>

## Beams are not wires

Take the metal away. Two beams of electrons fly side by side through a vacuum, the same way,
like the currents in two wires.

## What to carry forward

- **A current is circled by its field.** Right thumb along the current, fingers along the field; $B = \mu_0 I/2\pi r$, falling as $1/r$.
- **Currents the same way attract**, with $F/L = \mu_0 I_1 I_2/2\pi d$, but only because the wires are neutral.

Next: walk round a closed loop adding up the field, and find that only the current it wraps counts.
