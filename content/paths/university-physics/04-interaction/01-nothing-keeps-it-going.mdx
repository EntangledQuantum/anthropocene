A puck is sliding across frictionless ice at a steady 4 m/s. Ask almost anyone — including
people who passed a physics course — what is keeping it going, and they will reach for
something: momentum pushing it, a leftover force from the shove, impetus.

There is a way to settle this that does not involve arguing. Put a forward force on it and
watch what the world does.

## Your arrows, the world's answer

Here is the same puck with nothing on it but its weight and the ice. **Add a force.** Drag
its tip, or move the sliders, and then run it.

Two arrows in this picture are not yours. The dashed white one is the sum, and the thick
magenta one is the acceleration. You cannot drag either, because there is nothing there to
drag: they are arithmetic on the arrows you placed.

/>

Now do the experiment that settles the opening question. Get it up to speed, then **remove
your force while it is running**. The speed stops changing and stays exactly where you left
it. It does not sag back toward 4 m/s and it does not creep toward zero. Nothing is owed.

## The caption for what you just did

Add every force acting on your chosen body. Divide by the body's mass. That is the
acceleration, and there is nothing else to know:

$$\vec F_{\text{net}} = \sum_i \vec F_i = m\vec a$$

Two clauses in that sentence are doing quiet work. *Every force* — because forces
superpose, with no precedence and no winner; three arrows can add to nothing while all
three are fully present. And *on your chosen body* — because a force is something one body
does to another, so before you can add anything you have to say which body you are talking
about.

That is what a **free-body diagram** is. One body, every arrow that lands on it, and
nothing else on the page.

<Recall id="up-ch4-recall-inertia" concept="inertia">
  <div slot="front">A spacecraft is coasting between stars with its engines off. What is the net force on it, and what is keeping it moving?</div>

  Zero, and nothing. Constant velocity is the free state — it is what happens when the
  budget balances, and it costs nothing to maintain. The question "what keeps it going" has
  no answer because it presumes that motion is a thing needing upkeep. What would need
  explaining is a *change* in that velocity.
</Recall>

## Three arrows that add to nothing

Same idea in the opposite direction: instead of reading the acceleration off the forces,
supply forces that produce a required acceleration.

An 8 kg crate is drifting in deep space. Two thrusters are bolted on and fixed. The third
is yours — set its strength and its direction so the crate stops accelerating altogether.

  duration={8}

  caption="The magenta arrow is the thing to kill. Watch it shrink as you turn yours toward the south-west — and notice that it vanishes at an angle neither of the other two points in."

The answer is about 28 N at 225°, and it is the diagonal of the square the other two make.
Equilibrium is not the absence of forces; it is three arrows laid tip to tail that happen
to close. Every one of them is still there, still pulling, still able to tear a bracket off.

<Recall id="up-ch4-recall-fbd" concept="free-body-diagram">
  <div slot="front">You are drawing a free-body diagram and you want to add an arrow. What question has to be answerable first?</div>

  "Exerted by what?" Every arrow is one end of an interaction, so it has a second body at
  the other end that you can name: the Earth, the floor, a rope, the air, your hand. An
  arrow with no source — a "force of motion", a leftover from the shove — does not exist and
  belongs off the page. The velocity goes off the page too: it is not a force and cannot
  enter a sum of newtons.
</Recall>

## What to carry forward

- **Velocity persists; change costs.** A net force is the price of a *change* in velocity,
  never of velocity itself. The forward arrow on a coasting object has no one exerting it.
- **The diagram fixes the acceleration and hides the velocity.** One free-body diagram is
  compatible with moving fast, moving backwards, or sitting still. Read the net force as an
  instruction to the velocity, not a description of it.
- **Forces superpose as vectors, and equilibrium is a property of the sum.** Zero net force
  is a closed triangle of arrows, every one of them real.

Next: every arrow you drew has a partner you did not draw, on a body you did not choose —
and it is the reason a heavy object does not push harder than a light one.
