Two numbers from that run are worth putting side by side. The net force on each vehicle:
**600 kN, both of them.** The acceleration: **500 m/s² for the car, 43 m/s² for the truck** —
a ratio of 11.7, which is exactly 14 000 / 1200.

The forces were identical. The mass did all the discriminating, in the division.

$$\vec F_{\text{truck on car}} = -\,\vec F_{\text{car on truck}}$$

## Why the pair never appears on one page

You could not have dragged those two arrows apart. In the widget above, one force was
declared and the other was *derived* from it by swapping the two participants and flipping
the sign — because they are not two decisions, they are one interaction read twice.

What makes this useful rather than decorative is where the second arrow landed. It went on
the **other** body's diagram. A pair can never both appear in one sum, which is the only
reason anything in the universe is able to accelerate.

## The table does not owe you mg

That second item deserves its own experiment, because it is where the belief hides.

The normal force is not a rule with a value. It is whatever it takes to stop the block
accelerating into the surface, **clamped at zero**, because surfaces push and never pull.
Press down and it grows. Pull up and it shrinks. Tilt the surface and it becomes $mg\cos\theta$.
Pull hard enough and it runs out and the block leaves.

This is also what a bathroom scale reads — not your weight, but the force *it* is exerting
on you, whose partner is the force you exert on it. In a lift accelerating upward the two
disagree, and the number on the scale is the one that changed.

<Recall id="up-ch4-recall-pair" concept="third-law-pair">
  <div slot="front">Name the third-law partner of your weight. Where is it, and what is it doing?</div>

  It is your gravitational pull on the **Earth**, upward, equal in magnitude to your weight
  and acting on the planet. It is doing exactly what the second law says it should: dividing
  by six thousand billion billion tonnes and producing an utterly negligible acceleration.
  It is not the floor's push on you — that is a different interaction, with a different
  partner, and it is the one that disappears when you jump.
</Recall>

## Choosing what counts as the object

Two blocks sit touching on a frictionless surface: 2 kg in front, 6 kg behind it. You push
the 2 kg block with 16 N and both slide off together.

Now draw the boundary somewhere else. Put it around **both** blocks and treat them as one
8 kg object. The contact force and its partner are now both inside the boundary, they
appear in the sum twice with opposite signs, and they vanish. What is left is your 16 N on
8 kg, and the acceleration falls out in one line with no contact force ever mentioned.

That is the whole trick, and it is available in every problem for the rest of this path:

- **Want the acceleration of the pair?** Enclose both. The internal force cancels itself.
- **Want the contact force?** Enclose one. It becomes the only unknown left.

Same physics, two boundaries, two easy problems instead of one hard one. And it is why you
cannot move a car by pushing on its dashboard: that force is internal to the system you are
trying to accelerate, so it contributes exactly zero. Pushing on the *ground* works, because
the ground is outside.

<Recall id="up-ch4-recall-system" concept="system-boundary">
  <div slot="front">You choose to treat two touching blocks as a single system. What happens to the force each one exerts on the other?</div>

  It drops out. Each internal force enters the system's sum twice — once as itself and once
  as its third-law partner — so the two contributions cancel exactly and only external forces
  can change the system's motion. That is not an approximation or a simplification; it is
  what "internal" means. Choosing the boundary is choosing which forces you have to know
  about.
</Recall>

## What to carry forward

- **One interaction, two arrows, two bodies.** Equal magnitude, opposite direction, and
  never on the same diagram. No mass condition, no exceptions, no version of the law that
  fails when one object is bigger.
- **Equal forces, unequal consequences.** The car and the truck feel the same 600 kN. The
  wreckage difference is $a = F/m$ doing its work afterwards, and it is worth being able to
  say which of the two quantities you are actually talking about.
- **The normal force is a result, not a rule.** It supplies whatever stops the body
  accelerating into the surface, down to zero and no further.
- **The boundary is your move.** Redraw it and the awkward unknown either disappears or
  becomes the only thing left.
