<Step>

## Name it

The rule behind the tilt is the Lorentz transformation:

$$ct' = \gamma\,(ct - \beta x), \qquad x' = \gamma\,(x - \beta\,ct), \qquad \beta = v/c$$

Two events simultaneous on the platform are $\Delta t' = -\gamma v\,\Delta x/c^2$ apart on the
train, so the one ahead happens first. Times and distances change from frame to frame, but
$(c\Delta t)^2 - \Delta x^2$ does not: that is why the third meter never moved.

<Recall id="up-ch37-recall-simultaneity" concept="relativity-of-simultaneity">
  <div slot="front">Two strikes are simultaneous on the platform. For a passenger moving forward along the line between them, which comes first?</div>

  The one ahead, in her direction of motion, by $\gamma v\,\Delta x/c^2$. On a spacetime
  diagram her line of now tilts up toward the light line, so it meets the front event
  earlier.
</Recall>

<Recall id="up-ch37-recall-interval" concept="spacetime-interval">
  <div slot="front">Which two events can a moving observer see in the opposite order?</div>

  Only events outside each other's light cones, where $(c\Delta t)^2 - \Delta x^2 < 0$. That
  number is the same in every frame, so events that one could cause the other keep their order
  for everyone.
</Recall>

## What to carry forward

- **Simultaneous belongs to a frame.** For a mover, the event ahead happens first.
- **On a spacetime diagram a moving frame's worldline and line of now close like scissors** on the 45° light line.
- **The interval, and the order of events one could cause the other, are the same for everyone.**
