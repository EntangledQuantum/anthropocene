## Find the shove, not the height

Reveal force above. Sweep across both steps slowly. The upper tangent tilts most where the lower force curve reaches furthest from zero. On each nearly flat shelf, force nearly vanishes.

Now hunt on just the **right-hand step**. The high shelf is a tempting starting point; it is not the target.

For the smooth, one-dimensional **conservative forces** here, the landscape is a potential energy function $U(x)$. Its slope tells you the force:

$$F_x = -\frac{dU}{dx}.$$

The derivative is the [local rate of change](/anthropocene/learn/university-physics/motion-as-geometry/three-graphs/). The minus sign says **force points toward decreasing U**. It does not say a moving particle must travel downhill: force changes velocity; it does not replace it.

<Recall id="up-ch7-recall-height-slope" concept="potential-force-slope">
  <div slot="front">A high shelf and a low steep slope: which determines the stronger force, height or slope?</div>

  Local slope. A flat shelf has zero force regardless of its energy value. A steep slope gives a large force toward decreasing U. Read the tangent, not the altitude.
</Recall>

You choose the energy reference. Writing $U_{\mathrm{new}}(x)=U(x)+C$ changes the ledger's zero, not the interaction. Potential energy describes a configuration of an interacting system; it is not a force stored inside the particle.

<Recall id="up-ch7-recall-reference" concept="potential-reference-zero">
  <div slot="front">You add a constant to U everywhere. Which changes: energy values, energy differences, or force?</div>

  Only the energy values. The constant cancels in differences and has zero derivative, so it cannot change force or the motion from the same initial position and velocity.
</Recall>

## What to carry forward

Read a potential landscape with a tangent: **steep means strong; downhill gives the force direction**. Moving the energy zero never moves that tangent.

Next, use the energy difference between two positions to account for work, then add a total-energy line to ask where motion can reach.
