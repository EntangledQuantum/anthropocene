<Step>

## The line is the budget

Kinetic plus potential energy stays fixed, and the dashed line marks the total:

$$E = K + U = \text{constant}$$

Between the line and the rail lies the kinetic energy. Where the rail meets the line, $K = 0$ and the cart turns round.

<Recall id="up-ch07-recall-energy-line" concept="mechanical-energy-conservation">
  <div slot="front">A cart is let go from rest on a frictionless track. Why can it never climb higher than its start?</div>

  Its total energy is the U it started with. Higher up, U alone would exceed that total, which would
  need negative kinetic energy. The start height is a ceiling.
</Recall>

</Step>

## Friction lowers the line

A real track has friction somewhere. This valley has a brake strip on its flat floor.

Friction's work is the drop in the line, $\Delta(K+U) = -f\,d$, and that energy is now heat in the strip.

<Recall id="up-ch07-recall-friction" concept="nonconservative-work">
  <div slot="front">A cart loses 6 J of mechanical energy crossing a brake strip. Where did the 6 J go?</div>

  Into heat in the strip and the cart. Friction lowers the energy line by the work it does, and the
  thermal energy rises by the same amount. The total never changes.
</Recall>

## What to carry forward

- **The dashed line is the budget.** The cart reaches every point under it and turns where it meets the rail.
- **Between two heights, the path sets the timing, never the speed.**
- **Friction lowers the line by $f\,d$**, and exactly that much turns up as heat.
