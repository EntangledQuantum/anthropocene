You release two identical spring-mounted masses from rest. One starts twice as far from
the middle. It must travel twice as far to complete a swing. Does it arrive late?

You are going to build a clock, then find the change that breaks it. Here a **cycle**
means returning to the same position **and** the same direction of motion.

**One full cycle** to check the return, not just an accidental crossing.

## The circle explains the bargain

Watch the rotating point and its shadow. The point covers equal angles in equal times;
the shadow does not cover equal distances. Near an end, most of the point's motion is
up or down. At the middle, its motion is entirely sideways.

Set the release distance to 1 m. Compare **Reset experiment** with **Quarter cycle**.
At release, the mass is stopped but its acceleration is large. At the middle, it is
fastest but its acceleration is zero.

This is **simple harmonic motion**: acceleration points toward the middle and grows in
proportion to displacement. For an ideal spring, Newton's law gives

$$a=-\frac{k}{m}x=-\omega^2x,\qquad \omega=\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}.$$

The circle's horizontal coordinate names the curve you just moved:

$$x(t)=A\cos(\omega t),\qquad T=\frac{2\pi}{\omega}=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{m}{k}}.$$

$A$ is the release distance, $T$ the time for one cycle, and $\omega$ the angle swept per
second. This cosine starts at the right-hand turning point. Starting somewhere else
shifts the curve in time. We assume an ideal Hookean spring and no friction or driving.

## Make one clock lose

Now keep the release distance fixed and change **Moving mass** from 1 kg to 4 kg.
Scrub to the reference spring's first return. Your spring is at the opposite end.
More inertia slows the circle: four times the mass makes the period twice as long.

<Recall id="up-ch14-recall-spring-clock" concept="simple-harmonic-motion">
  <div slot="front">An ideal spring swings twice as far but keeps the same period. What else must double, and why?</div>

  The whole velocity history doubles. The restoring acceleration also doubles at each
  matching instant because $a=-(k/m)x$. Twice the travel at twice the speed takes the
  same time. The clock is set by $m/k$, not the release distance.
</Recall>

## Does every repeating swing share the clock?

Replace the spring with a pendulum. At small angles, its restoring acceleration is
almost proportional to angle. Try 10°, then 90° below. Use **One full cycle** and look
at where the hollow model bob is when the solid bob returns.

<OscillatorClock mode="pendulum" angle={10} />

At 10°, the period excess is about 0.19%. At 90°, it is about 18%. The real pendulum
still repeats; it is not simple harmonic motion at large angles.

The actual angular acceleration is $\ddot\theta=-(g/L)\sin\theta$. Replacing
$\sin\theta$ by $\theta$ makes a harmonic oscillator, with

$$T_0=2\pi\sqrt{L/g}.$$

That replacement is the **small-angle approximation**, not a law that all pendulums
keep perfect time. The error grows continuously; how small is “small” depends on the
accuracy you need. The solid trace integrates the sine force, not a slower cosine.

## What to carry forward

A bigger circle casts a bigger shadow, not a slower clock: that is the picture of ideal
spring motion. A pendulum borrows the picture only near the bottom. Next, follow where
the energy goes during a tick, before letting damping take some of it away.
