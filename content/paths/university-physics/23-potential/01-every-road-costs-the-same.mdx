<Step>

## Name it

The meter was reading a drop in height. Every place has a **potential** $V$, the potential
energy per coulomb a charge would have there, and the field's work is the charge times the drop:

$$W_{A\to B} = q\,(V_A - V_B), \qquad U = qV, \qquad V_{\text{point}} = k\,\frac{Q}{r}$$

It is chapter 7's landscape in new clothes: a height map for charge, measured in volts, where 1 V is 1 J/C.

<Recall id="up-ch23-recall-path" concept="electric-potential">
  <div slot="front">You carry 2 nC from a spot at 300 V to a spot at 100 V, by a long winding road. How much work does the field do?</div>

  +0.4 µJ, which is $2\,\text{nC} \times 200\,\text{V}$. The road does not enter: only the
  heights of the two ends.
</Recall>

</Step>

## Ski straight downhill

A new map, and only the map. The field at a spot is the push per coulomb on a + charge there.

<AimDownhill
  id="up-ch23-aim-the-field"
  prompt="Drag the arrow to point the way the field points at the white dot."
  explanation="Straight across the contour, toward lower ground. Along the contour V is flat, so the field does no work that way and has no part along it. Every orchid arrow now on the map crosses its contour square-on and runs downhill: E is the downhill slope of V, as the force was the downhill slope of U in chapter 7."
/>

<Recall id="up-ch23-recall-contour" concept="equipotential">
  <div slot="front">Why can a field line never run along an equipotential?</div>

  Moving a charge along an equipotential costs no work, so the field can have no component
  along it. The field always crosses equipotentials at right angles, pointing toward lower V.
</Recall>

</Step>

## What to carry forward

- **V is a height map for charge.** The field's work is $q$ times the drop in $V$, whatever the road; round a loop it is zero.
- **Contours are equipotentials**, and the field crosses them square-on, pointing downhill.
- **U = qV.** A negative charge sees the map upside down, and rolls uphill.
