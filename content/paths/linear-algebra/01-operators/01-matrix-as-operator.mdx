Five interior nodes on $[0,1]$. You already have a stencil for $-u''$:

$$
\frac{-u_{i-1} + 2u_i - u_{i+1}}{h^2} = f_i
$$

Poisson is $Au = f$. $A$ is $5\times 5$.

## A row is a stencil

The $i$-th row of $A$ *is* the three-point stencil, written at node $i$. Applying $A$ to a vector is applying that stencil at every unknown.

```ts
out[i] = (2 * u[i] - u[i - 1] - u[i + 1]) / (h * h);
```

That loop **is** $A$. There is no table behind it.

## The table hides the method

Same numbers. Switch the lab to **dense table** and grow $n$. At $n = 5$ you can still read the three bands. At $n = 24$ the table is a wall of zeros and the bands are a rumour. The spy still says the same sentence: a three-point stencil.

Sparsity is not a storage trick. The zeros are the operator refusing to see distant nodes.

## Wrap the rod

Identify the two ends. Each end stencil now has a neighbour: the other end. Two extra nonzeros appear in the corners of the spy, and every row has three entries.

Dirichlet $A$ is SPD: every eigenvalue is positive, $Au=b$ has a unique answer. How far that answer jumps when $b$ wiggles is $\kappa(A)$ — already named in [Conditioning versus stability](/anthropocene/learn/computational-physics/numerical-reality/conditioning-vs-stability/). For this Laplacian $\kappa$ grows like $1/h^2$ as you refine; that is the *problem* getting harder, not a solver failing.

<Recall id="op-stencil-card" concept="discrete-operator">
  <div slot="front">What is a matrix that came from a finite-difference stencil, and what does one row of it do?</div>

The matrix *is* the stencil, written once per unknown. Row $i$ of the 1D Laplacian reads $u_{i-1}, u_i, u_{i+1}$ and writes

$$
(Au)_i = \frac{-u_{i-1} + 2u_i - u_{i+1}}{h^2}.
$$

There is no second object behind the loop. A dense table of the same numbers is a costume that hides the bands.

</Recall>

<Recall id="op-sparse-card" concept="sparse-matrix">
  <div slot="front">Why is the 1D Dirichlet Laplacian sparse, and what is nnz in terms of n?</div>

Because the stencil is local: an unknown does not see distant nodes, so those entries are structurally zero, not "approximately small."

$$
\mathrm{nnz}(A) = 3n - 2
$$

for $n$ interior unknowns (three per interior row, two at each Dirichlet end). The zeros are the method. Storing $n^2$ slots would store the costume.

</Recall>

<Tier level="advanced" title="eigenmodes, the 2D spy, and the kernel">

The Dirichlet matrix $(1/h^2)\mathrm{tridiag}(-1,2,-1)$ is diagonalised by sines. On $n$ interior nodes of $[0,1]$,

$$
\lambda_k = 4(n+1)^2 \sin^2\!\bigl(k\pi / (2(n+1))\bigr), \qquad k=1,\ldots,n.
$$

The smallest is $\lambda_1 \to \pi^2$, the first eigenvalue of $-d^2/dx^2$. Switch the lab to $\sin(\pi x)$: the Rayleigh quotient $u^\top A u / u^\top u$ sits on that number. The eigenvector of $A$ *is* a discrete physical mode.

In two dimensions the five-point stencil

$$
\frac{4u_{i,j} - u_{i-1,j} - u_{i+1,j} - u_{i,j-1} - u_{i,j+1}}{h^2}
$$

is still local — five samples — but the spy is no longer tridiagonal. Number the grid row-wise and the north/south neighbours land $n$ columns off the diagonal. Two extra bands, far from the main diagonal, that are still next-door on the grid. Ordering is a costume; the stencil is the fact. (Fill-in during factorization is what happens when you *forget* that, and it is a later lesson.)

Periodic and pure-Neumann Laplacians share a kernel: constants, or, in 2D elasticity, rigid motions. The question "what physical mode is this eigenvector?" is the one to keep asking. A zero eigenvalue is a mode the operator cannot see.

</Tier>

## What to carry forward

$A$ is a stencil, not a spreadsheet. Sparsity is locality of the physics, written as structurally zero entries. Dirichlet makes this Laplacian SPD; periodicity gives it a kernel. How sensitive $Au=b$ is, once $A$ is SPD, is $\kappa$ — already named, not re-derived here.
