Two columns, almost the same arrow.

$$
A = \begin{bmatrix}
1 & 1 \\
\varepsilon & 0 \\
0 & \varepsilon
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Independent for any $\varepsilon \ne 0$. The inner products collapse them to a 2×2:

$$
A^{\mathsf T}A = \begin{bmatrix}
1+\varepsilon^2 & 1 \\
1 & 1+\varepsilon^2
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

The stretch of $A$ is $\kappa_2(A) \approx \sqrt{2}/\varepsilon \approx 1.4\times 10^8$ — a property of the pair, already named in [Conditioning versus stability](/anthropocene/learn/computational-physics/numerical-reality/conditioning-vs-stability/). Forming $A^{\mathsf T}A$ *squares* it. That extra stretch is the algorithm, not the question.

## A mirror, not an inner product

To triangularise $A$ you have to *lose a column* — zero everything below the diagonal in column 1, then in column 2. The stable way is a reflection, not a Gram matrix.

A Householder reflector is the mirror through the hyperplane orthogonal to $v$:

$$
F = I - \frac{2vv^{\mathsf T}}{v^{\mathsf T}v}.
$$

Choose $v = x + \mathrm{sign}(x_1)\|x\| e_1$ and $F$ sends $x$ onto the axis. The sign *enlarges* $x_1$ instead of cancelling it.

```ts
function house(x) {
  const v = x.slice();
  v[0] += (Math.sign(v[0]) || 1) * Math.hypot(...x);
  return v;
}
```

One $F$ per column. The product of the $F$'s is $Q^{\mathsf T}$. What remains is $R$. $A = QR$, $Q$ orthogonal, $R$ upper triangular. Orthogonal $Q$ does not stretch, so $\kappa(R) = \kappa(A)$.

## The same least-squares problem, two algorithms

$b = A\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix} = (2,\,\varepsilon,\,\varepsilon)$. Exact $x = (1,1)$. Two ways to ask for it:

- Normal equations: solve $A^{\mathsf T}A\, x = A^{\mathsf T}b$.
- QR: solve $Rx = Q^{\mathsf T}b$.

<Recall id="qr-kappa-squares" concept="qr-factorization">
  <div slot="front">What does forming AᵀA do to κ, and what does Householder QR do instead?</div>

$\kappa_2(A^{\mathsf T}A) = \kappa_2(A)^2$. Householder QR writes $A = QR$ with $Q$ orthogonal, so $\kappa(R) = \kappa(A)$. It triangularises without squaring the stretch.

</Recall>

<Recall id="qr-householder-mirror" concept="householder">
  <div slot="front">What does one Householder reflector do to a column, and why is the sign in v the whole trick?</div>

$F = I - 2vv^{\mathsf T}/v^{\mathsf T}v$ is a mirror. $v = x + \mathrm{sign}(x_1)\|x\| e_1$ enlarges $x_1$, so $F$ sends $x$ onto the axis without cancelling the leading entry. One reflection zeros a column below the diagonal. $R_{22}$ is the leftover orthogonal piece — order ε, not ε².

</Recall>

<Tier level="advanced" title="the sign, and the QR you should not run">

If you chose the other sign, $v_1 = x_1 - \mathrm{sign}(x_1)\|x\|$ would subtract two close numbers whenever $x$ is already near the axis — the lesson pair, in the construction of $v$ itself. Trefethen Algorithm 10.1 is the sign that makes $|v_1|$ large.

Classical Gram–Schmidt produces a $Q$ whose columns lose orthogonality once $\kappa$ is large; modified GS is better and still not backward stable for $R$. Householder (Lecture 16) *is*. This lesson owns the reflector, not Gram–Schmidt.

Column pivoting — swap in the remaining column of largest norm before each reflection — is how you *reveal* a rank that is actually gone. That is a different question: here the rank was never gone until you formed $A^{\mathsf T}A$.

</Tier>

## What to carry forward

Two arrows. Forming $A^{\mathsf T}A$ writes $\varepsilon^2$ against 1 and loses a column. A Householder reflector writes $R_{22} \sim \varepsilon$ and keeps it.

$\kappa(A^{\mathsf T}A) = \kappa(A)^2$. QR does not square it.
