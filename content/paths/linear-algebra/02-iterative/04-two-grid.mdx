## Two jobs, two grids

A local average cannot grab a wave that looks constant on three points. A grid twice as coarse sees that wave as a well-resolved sine. Complementary jobs:

1. Smooth on the fine grid — kill high-k.
2. Restrict the residual $r = f - Au$.
3. Solve $A_c e_c = r_c$ exactly on the coarse grid.
4. Prolong $e_c$ and correct $u \leftarrow u + P e_c$.
5. A post-smooth, because interpolation is not exact on what is left.

That is one two-grid V-cycle.

Prolongation $P$ is linear interpolation. Restriction $R$ is full weighting, the adjoint:

$$
(P e)_{2I+1} = e_I, \qquad (P e)_{2I} = \tfrac12(e_{I-1} + e_I),
$$

$$
(R r)_I = \tfrac14 r_{2I} + \tfrac12 r_{2I+1} + \tfrac14 r_{2I+2}, \qquad R = \tfrac12 P^{\mathrm{T}}.
$$

Walls contribute 0. On this Laplacian, Galerkin $R A^h P$ *is* the coarse stencil $A^{2h}$. The transfers are not a second discretisation.

## Nested two-grid is the V-cycle

The coarse problem is $A_c e_c = r_c$, the same leftover-wave Poisson. Smooth it, restrict to seven points, solve, prolong. Then again, down to one unknown. Recursion, not a new method.

<Tier level="advanced" title="n = 3, Helmholtz, and why AMG exists">

On $n = 3$, $P$ maps a scalar $c$ to $(c/2,\, c,\, c/2)$ and $R A P = 8$, the 1×1 coarse stencil $2/H^2$. Four numbers. The whole lesson.

A V-cycle is two-grid at every level, starting from $0$ on each residual. Work is $O(n)$ because the grids shrink geometrically; the contraction factor is the two-grid factor, independent of $h$.

Helmholtz is the costume that breaks. The operator is indefinite, so a local average is not a low-pass filter on the error, and the coarse problem is not easier. That is a later fight.

Algebraic multigrid keeps the two-grid idea when you have no mesh: the coarse “grid” is a subset of unknowns the matrix itself names. Same complementary split, no geometry.

</Tier>

## What to carry forward

High-k dies on the fine grid; low-k is a cheap coarse solve. Nested, that pair is the V-cycle. The two-grid cycle is already a cheap fake inverse of $A$ — the picture this chapter has been walking toward.
