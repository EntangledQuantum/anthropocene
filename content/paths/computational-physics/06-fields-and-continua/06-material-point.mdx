A Lagrangian mesh follows the material. That is the honest description of a solid, and it is how FEM works, and after enough deformation the elements invert. Snow, a landslide, a jelly hitting the floor — the Jacobian goes negative and the run dies.

The material point method looks like cheating: **throw the mesh away every step.** Keep the particles.

Occupied nodes follow the drop. Empty nodes stay. The cells at the free surface shear until a Jacobian goes through zero — unless you snap every node back to the lattice at the end of G2P. That snap is the method.

## Who owns what

A continuum solid wants two contradictory things from a mesh. The mesh must follow the material, so history has an owner. The mesh must stay nice, so $\nabla N_i$ stays well-defined. FEM picks the first and remeshes. An Eulerian grid picks the second and smears the first across cells.

MPM refuses the choice.

- **Particles** (Lagrangian): mass $m_p$, velocity $v_p$, deformation gradient $F_p$, rest volume. These persist.
- **Grid** (Eulerian, disposable): mass $m_i$, velocity $v_i$, internal force from $\nabla\cdot\sigma$. Used for one solve, then thrown away.

The transfer is the whole algorithm.

## Two particles, one cell

Bilinear hats in 2D, linear hats in 1D: $N_i(x) = 1 - |x - x_i|/h$ on a support of one cell. Inside a cell they sum to one.

$$
\sum_i N_i(x_p) = 1
\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad
\sum_i m_i = \sum_p m_p
$$

Mass on the grid equals mass on the particles after P2G. Momentum too. That is partition of unity, not magic.

It does **not** protect kinetic energy. Two particles of mass 1, velocities $+1$ and $-1$, in the same cell of width 1, at $x = 0.25$ and $x = 0.75$. Hats $0.75$ and $0.25$. After P2G the two nodes hold $v = \pm 1/2$. PIC G2P writes $v_p = \pm 1/4$.

FLIP does the other thing: it adds only the *change* in the interpolated field. With no grid forces that change is zero, so FLIP keeps $v_p$ and keeps the energy. The blend $\alpha$ interpolates.

$$
v_p \leftarrow (1-\alpha)\,v_{\mathrm{PIC}} + \alpha\,v_{\mathrm{FLIP}}
$$

<Tier level="advanced" title="cell-crossing, and why you do not get interpolation and conservation for free">

Bilinear hats are $C^0$. $N_i$ is continuous; $\nabla N_i$ jumps when a particle crosses a node. The internal force $f_i = -\sum V_0 P \nabla N_i$ therefore jumps, even if $F$ is smooth. That is **cell-crossing noise**, the original MPM artefact. GIMP, CPDI, and B-splines exist to give $\nabla N$ more support so the jump goes away. They are different transfers, not a different idea.

The deeper trade: a transfer that interpolates velocity exactly (PIC, particles recover $v_i$ at a node) is a projection, and projections dissipate. A transfer that conserves energy and angular momentum with a *lumped* mass (APIC: each particle carries an affine $C_p$, and P2G scatters $v_p + C_p(x_i - x_p)$) does not interpolate a general field exactly. You pick. Jiang, Schroeder, Teran 2016 is the paper; the one-line version is that **lumped-mass PIC cannot conserve angular momentum**, and the affine is what buys it back.

This lesson's drop uses bilinear PIC/FLIP and neo-Hookean elasticity. Snow (Stomakhin 2013) is the same transfers with a plastic projection on $F$. DEM and SPH are different animals: no borrowed grid.

</Tier>

## What to carry forward

History lives on particles. Derivatives live on a disposable grid. P2G, solve, G2P, reset — the mesh cannot tangle because it is not the same mesh tomorrow.

PIC filters, FLIP keeps the increment, and the blend $\alpha$ is a choice about energy versus noise, not about whether the solid still exists.
