## One Poisson solve

Any velocity splits uniquely (periodic, mean-zero potential) as

$$
u^* = u + \nabla\phi,\qquad \nabla\cdot u = 0.
$$

Take the divergence of both sides and you have Poisson: $\Delta\phi = \nabla\cdot u^*$.
Subtract the gradient. That is the Helmholtz projection. Pressure *is* $\phi$
(up to a factor of $\Delta t$).

On a staggered MAC grid the split is discrete-exact: curl of a gradient is
identically zero, div of a curl is identically zero.

```ts
div[i, j] = (u[i, j] - u[i - 1, j]) / h
           + (v[i, j] - v[i, j - 1]) / h;

// Δφ = div, then
u[i, j] -= (phi[i + 1, j] - phi[i, j]) / h;
v[i, j] -= (phi[i, j + 1] - phi[i, j]) / h;
```

Subtracting the mean velocity is not on that list. A constant is already
div-free, so removing it does not touch $\nabla\cdot u$. The projection
subtracts a *field* — the gradient of the Poisson solution.

<Recall id="proj-pressure-card" concept="pressure-projection">
  <div slot="front">What does the Helmholtz projection solve for, and what does it subtract?</div>

Solve $\Delta\phi = \nabla\cdot u^*$ for a mean-zero potential, then set
$u = u^* - \nabla\phi$. The result is divergence-free to the residual of that
Poisson solve — roundoff, if you actually solve it. Pressure is $\phi$ (times
density over $\Delta t$). It is the Lagrange multiplier of $\nabla\cdot u = 0$.

</Recall>

## The constraint belongs in the map

A pendulum that leaves the circle is not a slightly wrong pendulum. A velocity
that leaves $\nabla\cdot u = 0$ is not a slightly wrong fluid. Same structure,
different clothing — the [constraint as a map](../../structure-preserving/constraints/),
not a cleanup.

Chorin (1968) writes the incompressible step as: advance ignoring pressure,
then project. The Poisson solve is how the *next* velocity is required to
land on the constraint. Skipping it, or doing it "when div looks bad", is
integrating a different PDE.

<Tier level="advanced" title="Jacobi is not the projection, and Chorin is first order in time">

Iterative Poisson is how most codes actually invert $\Delta$. Each Jacobi
sweep locally averages the residual. The lowest mode of a periodic 5-point
Laplacian damps like $\cos^2(\pi/n)$, so leftover $\max|\nabla\cdot u|$ falls
slowly. Hunt the sweep count that first drops it to one percent of the start.

Collocated (non-staggered) $p$ and $u$ leave a checkerboard in the kernel of
the centred gradient, so Poisson cannot see it. MAC was Harlow and Welch's
answer in 1965: put the variables where the stencils make $\mathrm{curl}\,\mathrm{grad} = 0$
and $\mathrm{div}\,\mathrm{curl} = 0$ identities, not approximations.

Chorin's time split is first order: the predictor ignores $\nabla p$, so the
boundary sees the wrong pressure and a numerical boundary layer forms. Later
incremental schemes (van Kan, Bell–Colella–Glaz) put a lagged pressure in the
predictor. That is a better map. It is still a map whose job is to land on
$\nabla\cdot u = 0$.

</Tier>

## What to carry forward

Helmholtz: $u^* = u + \nabla\phi$ with $\nabla\cdot u = 0$. Pressure is the
multiplier of that constraint. If the discrete map does not land on it, the
next state is a compressible blob that happens to look swirly.
