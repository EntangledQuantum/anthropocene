Two cells on a ring. The left one holds $1$, the right one holds $0$. You move stuff across the shared face by writing **one number** into both updates: whatever the left cell loses, the right cell is told to gain.

That update, written for every cell:

$$
Q_i^{n+1} = Q_i^n - \frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\bigl(F_{i+1/2} - F_{i-1/2}\bigr)
$$

$Q_i$ is the **cell average**, not a point sample. $F_{i+1/2}$ lives on the face. Sum over $i$. Every interior flux appears once plus and once minus. Periodic: nothing remains. Discrete conservation is an **identity of the map**, not a hope about the PDE.

<Recall id="fvm-one-flux" concept="finite-volume">
  <div slot="front">What is stored in a finite-volume cell, and how is it allowed to change?</div>

A cell average $Q_i$. It changes only by the flux through its two faces:

$$
Q_i^{n+1} = Q_i^n - \tfrac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}(F_{i+1/2} - F_{i-1/2}).
$$

One number per face, handed to both neighbours. That is the whole method, before anyone has chosen a formula for $F$.

</Recall>

## Same stencil, different sentence

On a uniform line with $c > 0$, Rusanov's flux collapses to $F_{i+1/2} = c\, Q_i$. The update is

```ts
next[i] = Q[i] - nu * (Q[i] - Q[i - 1]);
```

which is the first-order upwind difference you already took past the [CFL](/learn/computational-physics/fields-and-continua/cfl/) cliff. Stay at $\nu = 0.4$. That question is closed. What is new is the *meaning*: cell averages and a shared face, so the sum is an identity. Finite difference wrote a derivative; it happens to telescope here.

## The chain rule is a smoothness theorem

Inviscid Burgers is

$$
u_t + \bigl(\tfrac12 u^2\bigr)_x = 0.
$$

If $u$ is smooth, the chain rule says that is $u_t + u u_x = 0$. Discretise the second sentence the way upwind taught you:

```ts
next[i] = Q[i] - lambda * Q[i] * (Q[i] - Q[i - 1]);
```

There is no $F$ on the face. Each cell uses its own $Q_i$ as the wave speed. At a jump the two cells disagree about what crossed.

## The shock is conservation in one number

A discontinuity of a conservation law moves at the Rankine–Hugoniot speed

$$
s = \frac{f(u_L) - f(u_R)}{u_L - u_R}.
$$

For Burgers, $f = u^2/2$, so a jump from $1$ to $0$ has $s = 1/2$. That is the same sentence as conservation form: the flux leaving the left state is what the right state receives, and the imbalance $[f]$ is what advances $[u]$.

A scheme in conservation form, if it converges, converges to a weak solution (Lax–Wendroff, 1960). A scheme that is not, at a shock, converges to the wrong jump (Hou–LeFloch, 1994). The telescoping is the reason.

<Recall id="fvm-telescoping" concept="conservation-form">
  <div slot="front">What makes a scheme conservative, in one line?</div>

One numerical flux per face, used by both cells. Then every interior $F$ appears once plus and once minus, the sum telescopes, and the global integral can change only at the domain boundary. Periodic: it cannot change. That is an identity of the discrete map, not a property borrowed from the PDE.

</Recall>

<Tier level="advanced" title="why the chain rule fails at a jump, and what Lax–Wendroff actually says">

For smooth $u$, $u u_x = (u^2/2)_x$ pointwise. For a jump, the distributional derivative of $u^2/2$ is $[f]\,\delta$, while $u$ times the derivative of $u$ is not a well-defined product. Any consistent discretisation of $u u_x$ inserts a Borel measure at the shock (Hou–LeFloch). That measure is the leak you watched, and it sets a shock speed that is not Rankine–Hugoniot.

Lax–Wendroff is an *if* theorem: conservative, consistent fluxes, and a boundedly almost-everywhere convergent sequence imply the limit is a weak solution. It does not prove convergence. It says that if you got a limit, conservation form is what made it a weak solution. Entropy is a further selection; this lesson stops before Riemann solvers, limiters, and WENO, which are how you pick $F$. The telescoping does not care which $F$ you picked, only that both cells used it.

On a non-Cartesian mesh the Cartesian coincidence with finite difference ends. The finite-volume unknown is still the cell average, the flux is still on the face, and the sum over cells still telescopes. That is why the method survives unstructured grids, which is the next costume of the same identity.

</Tier>

## What to carry forward

One flux per face, used twice. Then the sum is an identity of the discrete map — conservation is not a hope about the PDE, and it is not stability. Rankine–Hugoniot is that identity at a jump: $s = [f]/[u]$. Riemann solvers and WENO are how you pick $F$. The telescoping does not care which $F$ you picked.
