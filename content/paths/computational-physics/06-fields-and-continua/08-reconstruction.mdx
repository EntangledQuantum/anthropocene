A square pulse on a ring. [Finite volume](/learn/computational-physics/fields-and-continua/finite-volume/) stored the cell averages and moved them with one flux per face. Inside each cell the reconstruction was **piecewise constant** — first-order Godunov. The jump smears.

You can do better. Draw a line through each cell, read the faces off that line, flux those. Unlimited linear reconstruction. Second-order, on paper.

Godunov (1959): a **linear** scheme that never makes new extrema is at most first-order. Unlimited MUSCL is linear and second-order, so it must ring. The way out is a *nonlinear* switch on the slope.

$$
\delta_i = \mathrm{minmod}\bigl(Q_i - Q_{i-1},\; Q_{i+1} - Q_i\bigr)
$$

If the two one-sided differences disagree — you are at an extremum, or at a jump — minmod returns $0$ and the cell goes flat. If they agree, it takes the shallower one, so both faces stay between the neighbours.

```ts
delta[i] = minmod(Q[i] - Q[i - 1], Q[i + 1] - Q[i]);
face[i]  = Q[i] + 0.5 * (1 - nu) * delta[i];
next[i]  = Q[i] - nu * (face[i] - face[i - 1]);
```

Same conservation form. Same $\nu = 0.4$. The limiter is the only new line.

Piecewise constant: the face is $Q_i$. Piecewise linear: $Q_i \pm \tfrac12 \delta_i$. Unlimited $\delta_i$ is second-order and rings at a jump.

Total variation of the cell averages is

$$
\mathrm{TV}(Q) = \sum_i \lvert Q_{i+1} - Q_i \rvert.
$$

A scheme is TVD when $\mathrm{TV}(Q^{n+1}) \le \mathrm{TV}(Q^n)$. Sketch what unlimited Fromm does to that ratio.

## The slope that just touches

Three cells: $0$, $0.8$, $1$. Unlimited Fromm takes $\delta = \tfrac12(0.8 + 0.2) = 0.5$, so the right face is $0.8 + 0.25 = 1.05$ — past the neighbour. Hunt the largest $\delta$ whose faces stay inside.

<Recall id="rec-limiter-card" concept="slope-limiters">
  <div slot="front">What does a slope limiter do that a linear scheme cannot?</div>

It is a **nonlinear** switch: $\delta_i = \mathrm{minmod}(Q_i - Q_{i-1},\; Q_{i+1} - Q_i)$ (or superbee, MC, van Leer — any $\varphi(r)$ inside Sweby's region). Linear monotone schemes are first-order, by Godunov's theorem. A limiter makes the scheme nonlinear, so it can be second-order on smooth monotone data and flatten at a jump. TV cannot increase.

</Recall>

<Tier level="advanced" title="Sweby, extrema, and what WENO is for">

Sweby's second-order TVD region is $0 \le \varphi(r) \le \min(2r, 2)$ for $r > 0$, and $\varphi(r) = 0$ for $r < 0$. minmod is the lower boundary $\varphi = \max(0, \min(1, r))$. superbee is the upper boundary $\varphi = \max(0, \min(2r, 1), \min(r, 2))$. MC (van Leer) is the central slope clipped into that region. Any $\varphi$ inside is TVD and second-order on smooth monotone data.

TVD schemes are at most first-order at a *smooth* extremum (Osher–Chakravarthy). You saw minmod clip the sine's peaks. That is not a bug in the implementation; it is the theorem biting in a second place. WENO (Jiang–Shu 1996) is the reconstruction that refuses this: it weights several candidate stencils so the scheme stays high-order through a peak and still drops to upwind at a genuine jump. It is a sequel, not a limiter with a different name.

The two reconstructed states at a face still disagree — $u^L_{i+1/2}$ from the left cell, $u^R_{i+1/2}$ from the right. For linear advection with $c > 0$ the upwind choice is obvious. For a nonlinear flux it is not. That is the Riemann problem.

</Tier>

## What to carry forward

The function inside the cell is a guess. Unlimited, that guess rings at a jump — Godunov's theorem, because the scheme is linear. A limiter is the nonlinear switch that stops the slope at the neighbour, and then TV cannot increase. Next: the two face states still disagree, and a flux has to be chosen from that disagreement.
