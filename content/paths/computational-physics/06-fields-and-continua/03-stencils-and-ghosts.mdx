The interior stencil for $u''$ is the same three samples at every node that has both neighbours:

```ts
(u[i - 1] - 2 * u[i] + u[i + 1]) / (h * h)
```

At $x = 0$ that left sample is not in the domain. Poisson on $[0, 1]$, manufactured field $u = e^x$, right wall Dirichlet. Left wall is Neumann: you know the flux $\sigma = u'(0)$, not the value.

## Give the missing neighbour a job

The centred stencil at the wall wants a sample at $x = -h$. Nothing is there. Invent one.

$$
\frac{u_1 - u_{-1}}{2h} = \sigma \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad u_{-1} = u_1 - 2h\sigma
$$

That number is not a measurement. It is the Neumann condition, written as a neighbour, so the three-point stencil can sit at $x=0$ and stay itself:

```ts
uGhost = u[1] - 2 * h * sigma;
(uGhost - 2 * u[0] + u[1]) / (h * h)  //  =  f(0)
```

The residual of that wall operator, on the exact field, collapses only at one ghost value. Hunt it.

## Dirichlet already has the neighbour

Switch the left wall to Dirichlet $u(0)=\alpha$. The missing neighbour of the first interior stencil is sitting on the wall, and you know it. Plug $u_0=\alpha$ into the centred three-point at $i=1$. No fictitious point. The wall *is* the neighbour.

Naive here means something ruder: skip that value, pretend the missing sample is 0. You just solved $u(0)=0$, which is a different problem. Refining cannot fix the data you threw away.

An insulated wall is the same picture with $\sigma = 0$: $u_{-1} = u_1$, even reflection. Heat, a no-flux end, a symmetry plane — the missing neighbour is still the BC. The time-step restriction you already met in [When the grid explodes](../cfl/) is a property of the *interior* stencil, not of this wall treatment.

<Recall id="ghost-missing-neighbour" concept="ghost-points">
  <div slot="front">What is a ghost point, and why does the interior stencil get to stay?</div>

A fictitious sample outside the domain whose value is chosen so the boundary condition is true. For Neumann, $(u_1 - u_{-1})/(2h) = \sigma$ fixes $u_{-1} = u_1 - 2h\sigma$. The centred three-point then sits at the wall as if both neighbours existed. The missing neighbour is the BC.

</Recall>

<Tier level="advanced" title="the wall residual can look first-order and the solve still be second">

Substitute the Neumann ghost into the three-point at $x=0$ and Taylor-expand on the exact field. The wall truncation is $O(h)$, not $O(h^2)$ — the ghost is not $u(-h)$, it differs by $(h^3/3)u'''+\cdots$. Global max-error is nevertheless $O(h^2)$. One dirty equation, Laplacian inverse of size $O(1)$, and the pollution still drops a power of $h$ relative to a *naive* one-sided wall, which really is first-order globally.

That is the useful half of Gustafsson's principle: a boundary operator one order lower than the interior can be tolerated. It is not a licence to write $(u_1-u_0)/h=\sigma$ and call the method second-order. The ghost is the construction that actually delivers the extra order.

Once every wall is a ghost (or a known Dirichlet value), space has become a matrix. Time, if the PDE has one, is an ODE of size $n$. That is the method of lines. The CFL number you already dragged is how you step that ODE; it does not tell you how to fill $u_{-1}$.

</Tier>

## What to carry forward

The missing neighbour is a fictitious value that *is* the boundary condition. Dirichlet: the wall node, known. Neumann: $u_{-1}=u_1-2h\sigma$. One-sided is a different animal, and it is first-order. Keep the interior stencil; encode the BC as the neighbour it wanted.
