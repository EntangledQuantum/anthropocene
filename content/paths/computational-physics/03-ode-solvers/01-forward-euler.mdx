An **initial value problem** gives you the slope everywhere and the state at one moment:

$$
\deriv{y}{t} = f(t, y), \qquad y(t_0) = y_0
$$

You know which way the trajectory is heading from any point. You want the trajectory.

The obvious move is to trust the slope for a short while:

$$
y_{n+1} = y_n + h\, f(t_n, y_n)
$$

That is **forward Euler**, and it is the finite difference from the previous chapter
rearranged. It is also, in code, almost nothing:

```ts
export const forwardEuler = {
  step: (f, t, y, h) => axpy(h, f(t, y), y),   // y + h·f(t, y)
};
```

That is the actual implementation this page runs. Every curve below comes from it.

## Two kinds of error, and the factor that connects them

Taylor again. Over one step, assuming $y_n$ was exact:

$$
y(t_{n+1}) = y_n + h f(t_n, y_n) + \frac{h^2}{2} y''(\xi)
$$

So a single step is wrong by $\frac{h^2}{2} y''$ — the **local truncation error**,
which is $\Order(h^2)$.

But you do not take one step. To cross a fixed interval $T$ you take $N = T/h$ of them,
and the errors accumulate:

$$
\text{global error} \approx N \times \Order(h^2) = \frac{T}{h} \times \Order(h^2) = \Order(h)
$$

**The global error is one order worse than the local error.** That factor of $1/h$ is
the reason a method described as "$\Order(h^{p+1})$ per step" is called a $p$-th order
method. It applies to every integrator in this chapter.

## The failure that is not about accuracy

Here is the part that matters more than the order.

Apply forward Euler to $y' = -\lambda y$ with $\lambda > 0$. The exact solution decays
to zero. The numerical one satisfies

$$
y_{n+1} = y_n - h\lambda y_n = (1 - h\lambda) y_n
$$

so after $n$ steps, $y_n = (1 - h\lambda)^n y_0$. This decays only if
$|1 - h\lambda| < 1$, which means

$$
h < \frac{2}{\lambda}
$$

Cross that threshold and the computed solution does not merely become inaccurate — it
**grows without bound**, oscillating in sign, while the true solution decays quietly to
zero. There is no warning and no partial credit. It is a cliff.

That distinction is the whole reason the rest of this chapter exists:

- **Accuracy** asks how close you are, and improves smoothly as $h$ shrinks.
- **Stability** asks whether you are bounded at all, and fails discontinuously.

When $\lambda$ is large — a **stiff** problem — the stability limit can force a step
size thousands of times smaller than accuracy alone would need. You end up spending all
your computation resolving a transient that decayed to nothing in the first microsecond.
That is the problem implicit methods exist to solve.

<Recall id="euler-stability-card" concept="forward-euler">
  <div slot="front">For <code>y' = −λy</code>, what step size does forward Euler require, and is it an accuracy limit or a stability limit?</div>

The amplification factor per step is $1 - h\lambda$, so the method is stable only when

$$
|1 - h\lambda| < 1 \iff h < \frac{2}{\lambda}
$$

It is a **stability** limit, not an accuracy one. It does not soften if you are willing
to accept a worse answer — above the threshold the solution diverges regardless of your
error tolerance. For stiff problems (large $\lambda$) this forces an absurdly small $h$
and is the entire motivation for implicit methods.

</Recall>

and being able to tell it apart from a smooth divergence, is the thing that transfers.

<Tier level="advanced" title="the stability region, and why it is a picture in the complex plane">

The scalar test equation $y' = \lambda y$ looks too simple to say anything general. It
is not, and the reason is linearisation: near a solution, a system behaves like
$y' = Jy$ for the Jacobian $J$, and diagonalising $J$ decouples it into independent
scalar problems — one per **eigenvalue**. Those eigenvalues are complex in general.

So the right question is: for which complex $z = h\lambda$ does the method stay bounded?
For forward Euler the amplification factor is $R(z) = 1 + z$, and the **stability
region** is

$$
|1 + z| < 1
$$

a disc of radius 1 centred at $-1$. Two consequences fall straight out:

- Real negative $\lambda$: the disc reaches from $0$ to $-2$ on the real axis, recovering $h\lambda < 2$.
- **Purely imaginary** $\lambda$ — an undamped oscillator — lies on the imaginary axis,
  which touches the disc only at the origin. Forward Euler is unstable for *every*
  $h > 0$ on an oscillator. It does not merely lose accuracy; the amplitude grows without
  bound. You saw exactly this if you ran the oscillator problem above.

This is why "which method" is often decided by *where your eigenvalues are*, not by the
order of accuracy. A method whose stability region contains the whole left half-plane is
called **A-stable**, and no explicit method is.

## What to carry forward

Forward Euler is not a bad method because someone made a mistake. It is the honest
consequence of using the only information you have — the slope at the point you are
standing on. Everything that follows is an answer to a specific complaint about it:

- *first order is too slow* → sample the slope more than once per step (Heun, RK4)
- *the stability limit is intolerable* → evaluate the slope at the destination (implicit methods)
- *a fixed h is wasteful* → estimate the error and resize (adaptive stepping)
- *energy drifts on long runs* → preserve the geometry, not the trajectory (symplectic methods)
