Two clocks, one system. The slow one is the physics. The fast one died in the first millisecond. Take a step that looks generous for the slow clock.

$$
\dot y_s = -y_s + y_f, \qquad \dot y_f = -400\, y_f, \qquad y(0) = (1, 1)
$$

After $t \approx 0.02$ the fast mode is gone and the slow one is ordinary exponential decay. Forward Euler on that slow decay would be happy with $h = 0.05$. Take it anyway.

<Recall id="stiff-timescale-recall" concept="stiffness">
  <div slot="front">A solution is slow and smooth. An explicit method still needs a tiny step. What is going on, and what is the name for it?</div>

The Jacobian has a large negative eigenvalue — a fast transient that has already decayed. Explicit methods remain responsible for it: their stability region is bounded, so $h$ is forced below $O(1/|\lambda_{\mathrm{fast}}|)$ even though accuracy on the slow motion would have accepted something much larger.

That gap is **stiffness**. It is not a badly posed problem and it is not a rapidly changing solution. It is a timescale separation that explicit methods cannot ignore.

</Recall>

## Evaluate the slope where you are going

Forward Euler uses the slope at the point you are standing. The implicit move is to use the slope at the point you have not reached yet:

$$
y_{n+1} = y_n + h\, f(t_{n+1}, y_{n+1})
$$

The unknown is on both sides. That is the whole method. This is the code this page runs:

```ts
export const backwardEuler = {
  step: (f, t, y, h) =>
    newtonSolve(
      (guess) => guess.map((gi, i) => gi - y[i] - h * f(t + h, guess)[i]),
      axpy(h, f(t, y), y),   // explicit Euler as the opening guess
    ),
};
```

On $y' = -\lambda y$ the amplification factor is $R(z) = 1/(1 - z)$ with $z = h\lambda$. For every decaying problem and every positive step, $|R|$ stays below 1. There is no step-size stability limit. That is what you just watched.

It costs a nonlinear solve per step. The way you do that solve is not optional.

$$
y_{n+1} = y_n + h f(t_{n+1}, y_{n+1})
$$

Evaluating the slope at the *destination* puts $y_{n+1}$ on the right-hand side, so each step is an equation, not a formula. Fixed-point iteration of that formula contracts only when $h$ is already small enough for forward Euler. Newton on the residual is what keeps the method stable at the steps stiffness demands.

</Recall>

## The region that swallows the left half-plane

Every one-step method applied to $y' = \lambda y$ collapses to $y_{n+1} = R(z)\, y_n$ with $z = h\lambda$. The method is stable where $|R(z)| \le 1$. That set is a **region in the complex plane** — a shape, not an inequality.

Explicit $R$ is a polynomial, so $|R| \to \infty$ as $|z| \to \infty$, so the region is bounded. Implicit $R$ is rational, which is what lets it cover the whole left half-plane.

A method whose stability region contains every $z$ with negative real part is **A-stable**. No explicit method is. Dahlquist, 1963.

<Recall id="stiff-a-stable-recall" concept="a-stability">
  <div slot="front">What does A-stable mean, why is no explicit method A-stable, and what extra demand is L-stability?</div>

**A-stable:** the stability region contains the whole left half-plane — the method stays bounded on $y' = \lambda y$ whenever $\lambda$ has negative real part, at every positive step.

No explicit Runge–Kutta method can be: $R(z)$ is a polynomial, polynomials are unbounded, so $\{z : |R(z)| \le 1\}$ is a bounded island. Dahlquist’s second barrier says the same for linear multistep methods, and adds that no A-stable multistep method can exceed order 2.

**L-stable:** A-stable *and* $R(z) \to 0$ as $z \to \infty$. Backward Euler is; implicit trapezoid is not. The difference is a stiff mode that dies versus one that rings.

</Recall>

<Tier level="advanced" title="Dahlquist’s second barrier, and what production codes actually run">

Dahlquist (1963) proved three things about linear multistep methods at once:

1. No *explicit* multistep method is A-stable.
2. No A-stable multistep method has order greater than 2.
3. Among the A-stable order-2 methods, the implicit trapezoid rule has the smallest error constant.

That is why you cannot just “take more steps of history” and keep A-stability. The way out is to leave the multistep family: implicit Runge–Kutta (Radau IIA is L-stable at high order) or to accept a slightly smaller region — **BDF** methods of order 3–6 are $A(\alpha)$-stable, covering a wedge that is wide enough for the eigenvalues that actually appear. BDF2 is A-stable and L-stable; it is the workhorse inside CVODE and `ode15s`.

The practical question is the one this lesson opened with: *is my step small because the physics is fast, or because the scheme is explicit?* If the solution is slow and $h$ is being set by $\lambda_{\mathrm{fast}}$, switch family. Do not shrink $h$ by another factor of ten.

</Tier>

<Tier level="frontier" title="the same gap in other clothes">

Van der Pol at large $\mu$ is this lesson on a cubic nullcline: long slow drifts, then a fast jump. Robertson chemical kinetics is three species with rates spanning $10^6$. A spatial discretisation of $u_t = u_{xx}$ is a two-rate system with $\lambda_{\mathrm{fast}} \sim 1/\Delta x^2$ — that is why explicit heat-equation schemes have a CFL of $\tfrac12$, and why the first implicit method you met may have been called Crank–Nicolson (trapezoid, in disguise, and it rings).

In every costume the picture is the same. Left half-plane filled, or not. $R(\infty) = 0$, or not. Newton, or the iteration silently becomes the thing you were trying to avoid.

</Tier>

## What to carry forward

- **Stiffness** is a timescale gap. The fast mode is usually the boring one. Stability, not accuracy, sets $h$.
- **Backward Euler** evaluates the slope at the destination. The solve is Newton; fixed-point is forward Euler in a loop.
- **A-stable** fills the left half-plane. **L-stable** also kills $R(\infty)$. Trapezoid has the first and not the second, and you can see the ring.
