## Two solutions, one set of stages

You cannot compute the local truncation error — that would need the exact solution. You can *estimate* it: take the same interval two ways and subtract.

The smallest pair is Euler and Heun, sharing $k_1$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
k_1 &= f(t_n, y_n) \\
k_2 &= f(t_n + h,\; y_n + h k_1) \\
y_{\mathrm{Euler}} &= y_n + h k_1 \\
y_{\mathrm{Heun}} &= y_n + \tfrac{h}{2}(k_1 + k_2)
\end{aligned}
$$

Their difference is $\tfrac{h}{2}(k_2 - k_1)$: half a step times how much the slope changed. If the slope barely moved, the interval was easy.

An **embedded pair** is this idea with the stages already paid for. Two weight rows $b$ and $\hat b$ over the same $k_i$. The second solution is a few fused multiply-adds, not a second integration.

<Recall id="adapt-embedded-recall" concept="embedded-pairs">
  <div slot="front">What is an embedded Runge–Kutta pair, and why is the error estimate almost free?</div>

Two weight rows over the **same** stage evaluations, giving methods of order $p$ and $p-1$ (or $p$ and $p+1$). Their difference is the local-error estimate. You already computed the $k_i$; the second solution is a different weighted sum, not a different integration.

Dormand–Prince 5(4) — `ode45` in MATLAB, `RK45` in SciPy — is this: seven stages, two rows, advance with the 5th-order solution, estimate with the 4th. The 7th stage is FSAL (first-same-as-last), so an accepted step costs six $f$-evaluations, not seven.

</Recall>

Dormand and Prince (1980) chose the coefficients to make the *fifth*-order solution accurate, then used the fourth-order companion only as a check. You advance with the better answer. That choice is why this pair, not Fehlberg's, became the default.

## The controller, including the steps it throws away

Each step produces a scaled error

$$
e = \mathrm{rms}\!\left(\frac{|y_5 - y_4|}{\texttt{atol} + \texttt{rtol}\,|y|}\right)
$$

Target: $e \approx 1$. If $e > 1$ the trial was too big — **reject it**, shrink $h$, retry from the same point. The solution does not move. If $e \le 1$, accept and grow $h$.

Tighten the slider until magenta dots appear. They are part of the method.

The elementary resize is $h \leftarrow h \cdot (1/e)^{1/5}$, times a safety factor 0.9 so you aim a little under the tolerance rather than skating on it. A PI controller also looks at the *previous* error, so $h$ does not ring when the step sits near a stability boundary (Gustafsson 1988). That is what this page is actually running.

## What the two tolerances mean

`rtol` is significant digits. `atol` is "I do not care below this." The scale in the denominator is $\texttt{atol} + \texttt{rtol}\,|y|$ — whichever is larger wins.

On a solution of size ~1, `atol = 10^{-3}` and `rtol = 10^{-9}` is the same budget as `tol = 10^{-3}`. The tiny rtol never bites. Getting this backwards is the most common way to misuse `ode45`.

<Recall id="adapt-tol-recall" concept="adaptive-stepping">
  <div slot="front">What does a solver actually hold near the tolerance — local error or global error?</div>

**Local.** $e \le 1$ is a statement about this step, scaled by $\texttt{atol} + \texttt{rtol}\,|y|$. Global error is those locals accumulated and then amplified by the problem; it is typically larger, and no explicit RK pair stamps a digit count on $y(T)$. If you need a global bound, tighten the local tolerance and verify, or use a method that estimates global error on purpose.

</Recall>

<Tier level="advanced" title="Why the controller is PI, not just I">

The elementary rule $h \leftarrow h \cdot (1/e)^{1/5}$ treats the current error as if the problem were the same on the next interval. When you are limited by *stability* rather than accuracy — $h$ sitting on the edge of the stability region — that assumption is wrong, and $h$ oscillates: too big, reject, too small, grow, too big again.

Gustafsson's PI controller adds a term in the previous error, which damps that oscillation. SciPy's `RK45` uses a pure I-controller; Hairer's `dopri5` and SciPy's `DOP853` use PI. The pair is the same idea either way. The production detail is the memory.

On a rejected step you fall back to I-control: a stale previous error must not be allowed to *grow* $h$.

</Tier>

<Tier level="frontier" title="You do not adapt Verlet this way">

Changing $h$ every step on a symplectic integrator destroys the shadow Hamiltonian — the hidden nearby energy that was the whole point of using Verlet. Adaptive RK is the right default for a nonstiff IVP you want to some tolerance. It is the wrong default for a long orbit you want to stay an orbit.

Dense output is the other production footnote: Dormand–Prince comes with a polynomial that interpolates inside an accepted step, so you can plot a smooth curve without shrinking $h$ to the output grid. `ode45`'s `Refine` option is that interpolant, not extra steps.

</Tier>

## What to carry forward

- Local error ≈ the difference of two solutions on the **same** stages. That is an embedded pair.
- $h$ is a curve: tight where the slope is changing, loose where it is not. Using the smallest of those $h$ everywhere is how you waste the budget.
- `atol` and `rtol` are different questions. The scale is their sum; whichever is larger is the one you actually set.
