You measured this derivative twice. Both answers are too high. The coarser one is more too-high. Combining them can beat both — if you pick the right pair.

The expansion is even powers, because a central difference is symmetric:

$$
\begin{aligned}
D(h) &= f'(x) + c_2 h^2 + c_4 h^4 + \cdots \\
D(h/2) &= f'(x) + c_2 \tfrac{h^2}{4} + c_4 \tfrac{h^4}{16} + \cdots
\end{aligned}
$$

Four copies of the fine estimate minus one copy of the coarse one:

$$
\frac{4\, D(h/2) - D(h)}{3} = f'(x) + \Order(h^4)
$$

That is **Richardson extrapolation**. The same trick, iterated, is the tableau Romberg used for integrals.

<Recall id="rich-one-line" concept="richardson-extrapolation">
  <div slot="front">What combination of <code>D(h)</code> and <code>D(h/2)</code> kills the leading <code>h²</code> term of a central difference, and what order is left?</div>

$(4 D(h/2) - D(h))/3$. The two $h^2$ errors are in the ratio $4:1$, so that linear combination cancels them exactly and leaves $O(h^4)$. Iterating the same step on the new column cancels $h^4$, then $h^6$, and so on — that triangle of numbers is a Richardson tableau.

</Recall>

## The subtraction was the whole problem

Richardson still subtracts. The combination of two centrals *is* the five-point stencil, so the roundoff branch of the U-curve is still there — you just get a steeper truncation slope before you hit it.

If you never form a difference of nearby values, that branch has nothing to stand on.

If $f$ is analytic, Taylor works off the real axis:

$$
f(x + ih) = f(x) + ih\, f'(x) - \tfrac{h^2}{2} f''(x) - i\tfrac{h^3}{6} f'''(x) + \cdots
$$

Take the imaginary part and divide by $h$:

$$
\frac{\mathrm{Im}\, f(x+ih)}{h} = f'(x) - \tfrac{h^2}{6} f'''(x) + \cdots
$$

Second-order — and **there is no subtraction anywhere**. Squire and Trapp (1998) noticed you can then take $h$ down to $10^{-200}$ and still get full machine precision. Martins, Sturdza and Alonso later showed the imaginary part is a cheap cousin of a forward-mode AD seed, which is why aero codes used it to check Jacobians.

<Recall id="cstep-no-subtract" concept="complex-step-differentiation">
  <div slot="front">Why can the complex-step step size be <code>10⁻²⁰</code> without the error blowing up?</div>

Because the formula $\mathrm{Im}\, f(x+ih)/h$ never subtracts two nearby values of $f$. The U-curve's rising arm is cancellation, amplified by $1/h$. With nothing to cancel, shrinking $h$ only kills the $O(h^2)$ truncation term, and the estimate sits at machine precision.

The cost is real: $f$ must be analytic, and the implementation must actually accept a complex argument.

</Recall>

<Tier level="frontier" title="when the trick breaks, and what replaced it">

$f$ has to be analytic in a neighbourhood of $x$, and the code has to *see* the perturbation. `abs`, `min`, and `if (x > 0)` on a real-only path all break it — a branch cut sits on the real axis, or the imaginary part never enters. Martins, Sturdza and Alonso spent a paper on rewriting those operations so the method survives in a large CFD residual.

When you can instrument the source, reverse-mode automatic differentiation is the thing that replaced this: one reverse sweep for a whole gradient, exact to machine precision. Complex-step remains the check you run against an AD Jacobian, because it is independent.

</Tier>

## What to carry forward

- Two wrong answers with a known error expansion can cancel the leading term. That is Richardson. Iterating it is a tableau.
- Buying an order is not the same as removing roundoff. Richardson still subtracts, so the U remains.
- Complex-step never subtracts. The roundoff branch refuses to appear. The cost is analyticity.
