The definition of a derivative is a limit:

$$
f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h}
$$

A machine cannot take a limit. It can only pick some small $h$ and evaluate the
quotient. The entire question is: **which $h$**, and how wrong are you?

## Where the error term comes from

Taylor expand $f(x+h)$ about $x$:

$$
f(x+h) = f(x) + h f'(x) + \frac{h^2}{2} f''(x) + \frac{h^3}{6} f'''(x) + \cdots
$$

Rearranging for $f'(x)$:

$$
\frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h} = f'(x) + \frac{h}{2} f''(x) + \Order(h^2)
$$

So the **forward difference** is off by $\frac{h}{2} f''(x)$ — an error proportional to
$h$. Halve the step, halve the error. That is what *first order* means, and the
constant is not mysterious: it is literally half the second derivative.

Now do the same thing with a step in each direction and subtract:

$$
f(x+h) - f(x-h) = 2h f'(x) + \frac{h^3}{3} f'''(x) + \cdots
$$

Every even-powered term cancels by symmetry — including the $h^2 f''$ term that was
the leading error before. Divide through:

$$
\frac{f(x+h) - f(x-h)}{2h} = f'(x) + \frac{h^2}{6} f'''(x) + \Order(h^4)
$$

The **central difference** is second order, for the same two function evaluations.
That is not a small improvement. At $h = 10^{-4}$ it is roughly ten thousand times
more accurate, at identical cost.

## The curve that breaks the intuition

Watch what the error actually does as $h$ shrinks over fifteen orders of magnitude.

/>

The left half is the theory working perfectly — straight lines whose slopes are the
orders we just derived, 1 and 2 and 4.

Then every curve turns around.

The reason is the previous lesson. The numerator $f(x+h) - f(x)$ is a **difference of
nearly equal numbers**, and as $h \to 0$ they become more nearly equal. Each is known
to a relative precision of $\varepsilon$, so the absolute error in the numerator is about
$\varepsilon |f(x)|$ no matter how small $h$ gets. Dividing by $h$ then *amplifies* it:

$$
\text{roundoff error} \approx \frac{\varepsilon |f(x)|}{h}
$$

Total error is the sum of the two effects, and they pull opposite ways:

$$
E(h) \approx \underbrace{C h^{p}}_{\text{truncation}} + \underbrace{\frac{\varepsilon}{h}}_{\text{roundoff}}
$$

Differentiate, set to zero, and you get the optimal step and the best error you can
possibly achieve:

$$
h^{*} \sim \varepsilon^{1/(p+1)}, \qquad E(h^{*}) \sim \varepsilon^{p/(p+1)}
$$

For the forward difference ($p = 1$) that is $h^* \approx 10^{-8}$ and an error around
$10^{-8}$ — **you can never get more than half your digits back**, no matter how
carefully you choose $h$. For the central difference ($p=2$), $h^* \approx 6 \times 10^{-6}$
with error near $10^{-11}$.

<Recall id="fd-uc-curve" concept="truncation-roundoff-tradeoff" >
  <div slot="front">Why does making <code>h</code> smaller eventually make a finite-difference derivative <em>worse</em>?</div>

Two error sources move in opposite directions as $h$ shrinks:

- **Truncation** error $\sim C h^p$ comes from cutting the Taylor series short. It *falls*.
- **Roundoff** error $\sim \varepsilon/h$ comes from subtracting nearly equal numbers and
  then dividing by a tiny quantity. It *rises*.

Their sum has a minimum at $h^* \sim \varepsilon^{1/(p+1)}$. Past that point you are
paying more in lost significant digits than you gain in a better approximation to the
limit.

</Recall>

<Tier level="advanced" title="two ways to cheat the tradeoff entirely">

The U-curve looks like a law of nature. It is not — it is a consequence of *forming a
difference*. Remove the subtraction and the right-hand branch disappears.

**Richardson extrapolation.** If you know the error expands in known powers of $h$, you
can cancel the leading term algebraically. For the central difference,

$$
D(h) = f'(x) + c_2 h^2 + c_4 h^4 + \cdots
$$

Evaluate at $h$ and $h/2$, then take the combination that eliminates $c_2$:

$$
\frac{4 D(h/2) - D(h)}{3} = f'(x) + \Order(h^4)
$$

Two second-order estimates, combined, give a fourth-order one. Iterating this is
**Romberg** integration's differentiation cousin, and the same trick reappears in ODE
solvers as extrapolation methods.

**Complex-step differentiation.** This one is close to magic. If $f$ is analytic and you
can evaluate it in complex arithmetic, expand along the imaginary axis:

$$
f(x + ih) = f(x) + ih f'(x) - \frac{h^2}{2} f''(x) - \frac{i h^3}{6} f'''(x) + \cdots
$$

Take the imaginary part and divide by $h$:

$$
\frac{\mathrm{Im}\, f(x+ih)}{h} = f'(x) - \frac{h^2}{6} f'''(x) + \cdots
$$

Second-order accurate — and crucially, **there is no subtraction anywhere**. Nothing
cancels. So $h$ can be $10^{-200}$ and the estimate is accurate to full machine
precision. Turn the dashed curve on below and watch it simply refuse to bend.

The cost is real: $f$ must be analytic and implemented generically enough to accept
complex inputs. When that holds — and in scientific code it often does — the technique
gives derivatives at machine precision with one extra evaluation, and it was the
standard trick for verifying hand-written Jacobians before automatic differentiation
became routine.

</Tier>

<Tier level="frontier" title="why nobody does this in modern practice">

For gradients of real programs, finite differences have largely been displaced by
**automatic differentiation**, which computes derivatives by applying the chain rule to
the computation graph itself. AD is exact to machine precision, costs a small constant
factor over the function evaluation, and — decisively — computes a gradient in $n$
dimensions for roughly the cost of *one* function evaluation in reverse mode, where
finite differences need $n+1$.

Finite differences have not disappeared, though. They remain the tool of choice when:

- $f$ is a black box you cannot instrument (a legacy binary, an experiment, a vendor solver);
- you are **verifying** an analytic or AD gradient, where an independent method matters more than precision;
- you are discretising a PDE in *space*, where the finite-difference stencil is the
  numerical method rather than a way to estimate a derivative — and there $h$ is set by
  the grid, not by the U-curve.

That last case is the one that carries forward. The stencils here are the same objects
that appear in finite-difference and finite-volume PDE solvers; the difference is that
there, $h$ is a modelling choice, and the roundoff branch never comes into play.

</Tier>

## What to carry forward

- **Order of accuracy** is a slope on a log-log plot, and you should always measure it
  rather than trust it. A method claiming fourth order that measures 2.1 has a bug.
- **Symmetry buys accuracy for free.** The central difference costs the same as the
  forward difference and is an order better. That idea reappears constantly.
- **Smaller is not always better.** Every method with a subtraction in it has a floor.
