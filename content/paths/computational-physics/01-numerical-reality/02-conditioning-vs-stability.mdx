$$
\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1+10^{-8} \end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix}
=

$$
\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1+10^{-8} \end{bmatrix}

That stretch has a name. For $Ax=b$,

$$
\kappa_\infty(A) = \|A\|_\infty\,\|A^{-1}\|_\infty
$$

which for this matrix is $(2+\varepsilon)^2/\varepsilon \approx 4\times 10^8$. A backward-stable algorithm — LU with partial pivoting is one — returns the **exact** answer to a **nearby** question. The nearby question is $O(\varepsilon)$ away. Stretch that by $\kappa$ and you have the error you actually see:

$$
\frac{\|\hat x - x\|}{\|x\|} = O(\kappa\,\varepsilon).
$$

<Recall id="cond-kappa-stretch" concept="conditioning">
  <div slot="front">What is κ, geometrically, and whose property is it?</div>

κ is the **stretch** of a small disk of inputs into the corresponding set of outputs. For $Ax=b$ it is $\|A\|\,\|A^{-1}\|$. It belongs to the *problem*, not the method: every algorithm that answers this question inherits the same stretch.

</Recall>

## Same roots, two algorithms

The 2×2 was a bad question. Here is a good question asked badly.

$$
x^2 + 10^8 x + 1 = 0
$$

has roots near $-10^8$ and $-10^{-8}$, both simple. Simple roots are well-conditioned: nudge the coefficients a little and the roots move a little.

$$
x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}
$$

The stable evaluation is one extra line:

```ts
const q = -0.5 * (b + Math.sign(b) * Math.sqrt(b * b - 4 * a * c));
const far  = q / a;       // no cancellation
const near = c / q;       // Vieta: x1 x2 = c/a
```

<Recall id="cond-backward-slogan" concept="numerical-stability">
  <div slot="front">A backward-stable algorithm gives what kind of answer to what kind of question?</div>

**Exactly** the right answer to **nearly** the right question.

The output $\hat x$ is the exact solution of a problem whose input sits $O(\varepsilon)$ from the one you typed. Whether that is good enough is then entirely a question about $\kappa$ — about how much that nearby problem can differ in its answer.

</Recall>

<Tier level="advanced" title="forward error, backward error, and the one bound">

Three distances, not one.

- **Forward error** $\|\hat x - x\|/\|x\|$ — how wrong is the answer. What you wanted to know, and usually cannot measure.
- **Backward error** — the smallest $\delta A, \delta b$ such that $(A+\delta A)\hat x = b+\delta b$. For LU with partial pivoting this is $O(\varepsilon)$. You *can* measure it: it is the residual, up to a factor of $\|A\|$.
- **Conditioning** $\kappa$ — how the problem stretches backward error into forward error.

Trefethen's theorem 15.1 is the whole subject in one line: a backward-stable algorithm on a problem with condition number $\kappa$ satisfies

$$
\frac{\|\tilde f(x) - f(x)\|}{\|f(x)\|} = O\bigl(\kappa(x)\,\varepsilon\bigr).
$$

Wilkinson's polynomial $(x-1)(x-2)\cdots(x-20)$ is the 2×2 in a different costume. Perturbing a coefficient by $2^{-23}$ throws roots off the real axis. `np.roots` is stable. The *question* (recover those roots from monic coefficients) is not.

</Tier>

<Tier level="frontier" title="what 'nearby' is allowed to mean">

Normwise backward stability is the coarse picture. Higham's finer one asks for a *componentwise* nearby problem: each entry of $A$ and $b$ perturbed relatively, not just the norms. Mixed forward-backward stability allows a little of both. Most of the algorithms you actually run (Householder QR, LU with partial pivoting, the rearranged quadratic) are backward stable in the normwise sense and often stronger.

The slogan does not change. An unstable algorithm is one whose output is not the answer to any problem you would still recognise as yours.

</Tier>

## What to carry forward

κ is a stretch of the input disk. It belongs to the question. A stable algorithm lands inside a nearby disk; an unstable one does not.

Forward error ≲ κ × backward error. Extra digits buy more of that budget. They do not shrink κ, and they do not rescue a cancellation you can algebraically remove.
