The solver is a function $\theta \mapsto y(t;\theta)$. Distance to the target is a
scalar

$$
L(\theta) = \tfrac{1}{2} N^{-1}\sum_n \bigl(y_n(\theta) - y_n^\star\bigr)^2
$$

and $\nabla L$ is a direction. You just watched gradient descent follow it.

## One reverse sweep, not n

Finite-difference the whole solver and you pay one extra forward run per
parameter. Reverse-mode AD — the discrete adjoint — seeds the scalar $L$ and
sweeps the computational graph **backward**. The whole gradient then costs a
small constant times one evaluation, however many parameters you have.

## The reverse sweep is the stepper, backwards

For $y' = -\lambda y$ under forward Euler, $y_{\{n+1\}} = (1 - h\lambda)\, y_n$.
The reverse sweep is those two multiplies, read right to left:

```ts
// adj[n] already holds dL/dy_n from the mismatch
for (let n = N - 1; n >= 0; n--) {
  dLambda += adj[n + 1] * (-h * y[n]);   // ∂y_{n+1}/∂λ
  adj[n]  += adj[n + 1] * (1 - h * lambda); // ∂y_{n+1}/∂y_n
}
```

RK4 is the same chain rule with four stages. The platform's adjoint and a
complex-step of the same trajectory agree to roughly $10^{-8}$; that check is
the reason the Richardson lesson taught you a derivative with no subtraction.
You do not re-derive it here — you **use** it as the independent probe.

<Recall id="ad-reverse-one-line" concept="automatic-differentiation">
  <div slot="front">When is reverse-mode cheaper than finite-differencing a solver, and by what factor?</div>

Whenever the quantity of interest is a **scalar** (a loss, a drag, a likelihood)
and you have more than a few parameters. Reverse-mode is one extra reverse
sweep, cost $\sim 2$–$4$ forwards, independent of $n$. Finite differences cost
$n+1$ forwards. At $n = 200$ that is two orders of magnitude.

</Recall>

## Memory is a choice

Chen, Rubanova, Bettencourt and Duvenaud (2018) made this reverse sweep famous
as a *Neural ODE*: parameterise $f_\theta$, let a black-box solver produce
$y(T)$, train $\theta$ by an adjoint ODE solved backward from $\partial L/\partial y(T)$.
The advertised trick is memory. You do not store the forward trajectory — you
reconstruct it by integrating $f$ backward from $y(T)$.

The same reverse sweep recovers $\omega$ in $\ddot q = -\omega^2 q$. Switch the
lab to the oscillator, drag the target, descend. A long window makes that loss
non-convex — trajectories wrap and a far-off $\omega$ can look locally better.
Inverse problems are ill-posed; regularisation is a method, not a disclaimer.
The short window in the widget is the well-posed costume of the same idea.

<Tier level="frontier" title="PINNs: the loss is a residual you can see">

A physics-informed network does not replace the solver. It replaces the
**unknown function** with a network $\hat u_\theta(x,t)$, and it trains $\theta$
by making the PDE residual small at collocation points:

$$
\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \bigl\| \hat u_t + \mathcal{N}[\hat u] \bigr\|^2 + \text{data / IC / BC}
$$

That is Raissi, Perdikaris, Karniadakis (2019). The residual is the loss. If
you drop it, you have interpolation, and interpolation is not a solution.

A classical RK4 step on this ODE has a residual at roundoff. The network is
doing something else: it is **optimising** a residual. Sometimes that is the
right tool — irregular domains, inverse coefficients mixed into $\theta$, a
fast surrogate you will actually verify. Sometimes it is cognitive offloading
with extra steps. The product rule from the thesis still applies: do not lift
the residual unless you are still checking it.

<Recall id="ad-pinn-checks-residual" concept="physics-informed-nn">
  <div slot="front">What does a PINN actually minimise, and why is a pretty field not evidence it worked?</div>

The loss is the PDE residual (plus data / IC / BC), evaluated at collocation
points. A pretty field is a statement about values. The equation is a statement
about derivatives. Those can disagree, and the residual plot is how you find
out. If you do not plot it, you did not solve anything.

</Recall>

## What to carry forward

- A solver is a map from parameters to a trajectory. Reverse-mode through that
  map is one extra reverse sweep when the mismatch is a scalar.
- The two pictures to keep: two curves locking as a marker slides down a bowl;
  a residual plot that does not go quiet just because the values matched.
- Complex-step (already yours) is the independent check of the adjoint. A PINN
  that is not checked against a residual is a fit, not a solve.
