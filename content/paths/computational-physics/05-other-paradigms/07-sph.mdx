A cup of water sloshes. A Lagrangian mesh that follows it tangles. An Eulerian mesh that stays put needs a free-surface tracker. You already know how [molecular dynamics](../molecular-dynamics/) treats a pair potential, and what a [PDE](../../fields-and-continua/from-particles-to-fields/) stores on a grid. Throw the grid away. The particles *are* the water. How do you even know the density?

$$
\rho_i = \sum_j m_j\, W\bigl(|x_i - x_j|,\, h\bigr)
$$

including $j = i$. The smoothing length $h$ is the resolution. There is no connectivity to invert.

On a 1D lattice the cubic spline recovers a constant *exactly* when the nearest neighbours sit at $q = r/h = 1$.

A free surface truncates the same kernel. Mass did not leave. Neighbours on the empty side did.

The force is the gradient of that same $W$. Pairwise, so Newton's third law is an identity, not a hope.

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}v_i}{\mathrm{d}t}
= -\sum_j m_j\Bigl(\frac{P_i}{\rho_i^2} + \frac{P_j}{\rho_j^2}\Bigr)\nabla_i W_{ij}
+ g
$$

Weakly compressible: $P = c_0^2(\rho - \rho_0)$. Negative density error, negative pressure.

<Recall id="sph-one-line" concept="sph">
  <div slot="front">How does SPH estimate density, and what is the price of having no mesh?</div>

$\rho_i = \sum_j m_j W(|x_i-x_j|, h)$. The kernel *is* the cell — no connectivity, so a splash cannot tangle. Completeness fails at a free surface (missing neighbours, not missing mass). The same $W$ whose gradient gives the pairwise force has $W''$ that makes tension a negative modulus: particles clump. That is the tensile instability.

</Recall>

<Tier level="advanced" title="Wendland, artificial stress, and when to keep the grid">

Swegle, Hicks, Attaway (1995): von Neumann analysis of 1D SPH. Unstable when $\sigma W'' > 0$ (their sign convention). For the cubic B-spline, $W''$ is negative near the origin and positive past the inflection, so a tensile stress is an imaginary sound speed. Artificial viscosity does not remove it — it is not a time-integration artefact.

Monaghan’s tensile correction (2000) adds a short-range repulsive stress when $P < 0$. Wendland kernels (Dehnen & Aly 2012) have no pairing instability at large neighbour count because their Fourier transform stays non-negative; they are what you switch to when you wanted more neighbours for convergence and the cubic spline started clumping.

Incompressible SPH replaces the EOS with a pressure Poisson solve — now you *do* have an elliptic problem, and you are halfway back to a grid method. Weakly compressible SPH (this lesson) keeps $P = c_0^2(\rho-\rho_0)$ and pays with a sound-speed CFL.

Boundaries: dummy particles (this lab), ghost particles, or a repulsive wall force. All of them exist because a truncated kernel is a zeroth-order error. Shepard filtering and RKPM restore completeness; they are cousins, not SPH.

When the grid is simpler: confined flow with a stable free-surface tracker, or any elliptic constraint you were going to solve anyway. When SPH is simpler: astrophysical gas, violent free surfaces, fragmentation. MPM keeps a *disposable* grid so it can take derivatives without $\nabla W$; SPH never borrows one.

</Tier>

## What to carry forward

Density is $\sum m W$. No mesh, so nothing to tangle — and nothing to guarantee completeness at a free surface.

The kernel that estimates $\rho$ is the kernel that clumps in tension. That is the deal.
