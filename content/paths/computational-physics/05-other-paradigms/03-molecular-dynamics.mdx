Thirty-six particles, a pair potential, tens of thousands of steps. RK4 is fourth order. Velocity Verlet is second. You already know which one holds energy on an oscillator. Does that still pick the method when the "orbit" is a gas?

The symplectic argument is owned by the velocity-Verlet lesson. This one spends it. The force is a gradient of a pair potential, the Hamiltonian is separable, and you are about to ask the trajectory for thermodynamic numbers. Use the map that has a shadow Hamiltonian.

```ts
const a  = accel(q);
const qNext = q + h * v + 0.5 * h * h * a;
const aNext = accel(qNext);
const vNext = v + 0.5 * h * (a + aNext);
```

One force evaluation if you carry `a` forward. That is why MD packages speak Verlet and almost never RK4.

</Predict>

The potential is Lennard-Jones, in reduced units $\varepsilon = \sigma = 1$:

$$
V(r) = 4\left[\left(\tfrac{1}{r}\right)^{12} - \left(\tfrac{1}{r}\right)^{6}\right]
$$

Hard core, attractive well at $r = 2^{1/6}$, nothing at long range. The box is periodic: each pair interacts through the **minimum-image** vector, the shortest displacement on the torus, not the one you see drawn in the square.

There is no temperature knob on Newton's laws. After enough steps, the kinetic energy fluctuates around a number. In 2D, with $k_B = 1$,

$$
T = \frac{K}{N}, \qquad K = \tfrac12\sum_i |v_i|^2
$$

That number *is* the temperature. Pressure is the same idea with forces in it: kinetic plus virial,

$$
P = \rho T + \tfrac{1}{2} A^{-1}\sum_{i<j} \mathbf{r}_{ij}\cdot\mathbf{F}_{ij}
$$

The first term is the ideal gas. The second is the interactions — positive when cores push, negative when the well pulls. Thermodynamics is the time average of these traces, not any one frame.

The gas above is NVE: number, volume, energy. Verlet's shadow Hamiltonian pins E, so T and P fluctuate around whatever that energy implies. You do not choose T. You choose the initial condition and you wait.

Most of the time that is not what you wanted. You wanted T = 0.8, not "whatever this trajectory is at." So you change the equations.

A thermostat is not a better integrator. It is a different physical system: the particles, plus a coupling to a bath. Verlet still advances $q$ and $v$. The coupling is extra.

<Recall id="md-thermostat-modifies-dynamics" concept="molecular-dynamics">
  <div slot="front">What does a thermostat change — the integrator, or the equations of motion — and what happens to the energy Verlet was conserving?</div>

A thermostat changes the **equations**. Verlet is still the stepper. You have coupled the particles to a bath, so the discrete map is no longer the symplectic NVE map, and the mechanical energy $K+V$ is no longer the conserved quantity of that map.

Velocity rescaling has no replacement conserved quantity. Nosé–Hoover does: an extended Hamiltonian $H + \tfrac12 Q\xi^2 + g T_0 \ln s$, which is a different object from $H$. In either case, thermodynamics from mechanics now means a time average of a *modified* dynamics.

</Recall>

<Tier level="frontier" title="Nosé–Hoover, cutoffs, and what MD actually spends">

The thermostat in the widget is Berendsen rescaling — the honest teaching thermostat, because you can see the map break. Production NVT uses **Nosé–Hoover** (or a chain, or stochastic Langevin). Nosé–Hoover extends the phase space with a friction $\xi$ whose equation of motion is $\dot\xi = (2K - g T_0)/Q$. The original energy fluctuates, as a canonical ensemble must. The extended energy is the conserved object, provided you integrate the extended system with a method that knows about it. A naive "Verlet plus a $\xi$ kick" is first-order in the bath; geometric NH integrators exist and are what the packages run.

The other costs, once the integrator is not the question:

- **Neighbours.** The pair loop is $\Order(N^2)$. Cell lists and Verlet neighbour lists bring it to $\Order(N)$ past a cutoff (usually $2.5\sigma$). The cutoff is a discontinuity; a shifted potential at least makes $V(r_c)=0$.
- **Minimum-image** is unique only for $r_c \le L/2$. Larger boxes, or Ewald / PPPM / FMM, once the potential is long-ranged (charges, gravity).
- **Constraints** (bonds, SHAKE/RATTLE) because the stiffest modes set $h$, and MD is stiff in spirit: the $r^{-13}$ core dwarfs everything else.
- **The question to keep asking:** what is the conserved energy of the *discrete map*, not of nature? NVE Verlet: a shadow Hamiltonian near $H$. Thermostatted MD: either nothing (rescaling) or an extended $H$ (Nosé–Hoover). Measuring $T$ from a map that is silently bleeding $H$ is how you publish a wrong melting point.

</Tier>

## What to carry forward

- Temperature and pressure are time averages of a trajectory. In NVE that trajectory is a symplectic map; the averages are thermodynamics of the energy you started with.
- A thermostat is a modification of the dynamics. It buys you a chosen $T$ by giving up the original conserved energy.
- The next time a solver offers "NVE" and "NVT" as dropdowns, read them as two different equations of motion, not two post-processors of the same run.
