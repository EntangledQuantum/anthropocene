Ten dimensions. Two points per axis. That is a midpoint grid of $2^{10} = 1024$
evaluations — every cell centre of a $2\times 2\times\cdots\times 2$ lattice. You
have the same budget of 1024 uniform random samples of the same smooth function,
$f(x) = \exp(\sum_i x_i)$ on the unit cube.

The grid is a second-order rule. The samples have no order at all. Which relative
error is smaller?

A one-dimensional midpoint has error $\Order(h^2)$. In $d$ dimensions you take $n$
points per axis, so $N = n^d$ and $h = N^{-1/d}$. The same $\Order(h^2)$ is

$$
\Order(N^{-2/d})
$$

At $d = 4$ that exponent is already $-1/2$. Past that, it is shallower than Monte
Carlo's.

Monte Carlo does not have an $h$. It has a sample mean:

```ts
function monteCarlo(f, dim, N, rng) {
  let sum = 0;
  const x = new Array(dim);
  for (let i = 0; i < N; i++) {
    for (let k = 0; k < dim; k++) x[k] = rng();
    sum += f(x);
  }
  return sum / N;
}
```

The error of a mean is $\sigma/\sqrt{N}$. The Central Limit Theorem does not know
what $d$ is. Caflisch's one-line summary: that independence is why Monte Carlo is
the only viable method for a wide range of high-dimensional integrals, and also
why it is slow.

<Recall id="mc-rate-recall" concept="monte-carlo-integration">
  <div slot="front">Why does Monte Carlo beat a product grid in high dimension, even though its error only falls as 1/√N?</div>

A product rule of one-dimensional order $p$ has error $\Order(N^{-p/d})$, because
$N = n^d$ so $h = N^{-1/d}$. Monte Carlo's RMSE is $\sigma/\sqrt{N}$ in every
dimension — the CLT does not see $d$. Once $d > 2p$ the Monte Carlo exponent is
already steeper, and a few dimensions later the grid cannot even spend a typical
budget (two points per axis is $2^d$ evaluations). Variance is the remaining
cost; it lives in $\sigma$, not in the exponent.

</Recall>

The constant $\sigma$ *can* grow with dimension, and it is the one thing you can
attack. Importance sampling draws from a density $q$ closer to where $f$ is large,
and averages $f/q$ instead of $f$. Unbiased for any $q$ that charges the cube.
Still a sample mean. Still $\Order(N^{-1/2})$. The line drops. It does not rotate.

attack $\sigma$, leave the exponent alone.

<Tier level="frontier" title="quasi-Monte Carlo, and why it is not a free 1/N">

Replace the random points with a low-discrepancy sequence — Halton, Sobol, a
lattice. The Koksma–Hlawka inequality then bounds the error by
$V(f)\, D_N^*$, and the star-discrepancy of these sequences is
$\Order((\log N)^d / N)$. Closer to $1/N$ than $1/\sqrt{N}$, on paper.

Two catches, both real. The $(\log N)^d$ eventually explodes, so in genuine
high dimension the bound is worse than Monte Carlo's. And the gain you actually
see lives in integrands of **low effective dimension** — most of the variance
sits in a few coordinates or a few-way interactions (Caflisch, Morokoff, Owen).
Reparametrize so that is true (Brownian bridge, PCA of a covariance), and
quasi-MC can look like $N^{-1}$ on a finance problem that is nominally
256-dimensional.

It is still quadrature of a function you can evaluate. It is not a Markov
chain. Metropolis is a later lesson, for when you cannot write $f$ down, only
the ratio $f(x')/f(x)$.

</Tier>

## What to carry forward

- A product rule of order $p$ is order $p/d$ in $d$ dimensions. Monte Carlo is
  order $1/2$ in every dimension. That crossover is why high-dimensional
  integration throws darts.
- Variance is the cost. Importance sampling, control variates, antithetic pairs —
  all of them buy a smaller $\sigma$, never a better exponent.
- Next: when the measure itself is the hard part, not the integral of a function
  you can evaluate.
