A hundred electrons. You already know how [molecular dynamics](../molecular-dynamics/) treats a pair potential: loop over pairs, Newton's third law, Verlet. Coulomb is a pair potential. So — do you write the $N^2$ loop?

Toggle **plasma**. Particles, ρ, and φ are one state. A small displacement of a cold electron sea against a fixed ion background — the density rings, and the field that rings it came from a Poisson solve, not from a sum over pairs.

The [PDE](../../fields-and-continua/from-particles-to-fields/) that sits on that mesh is Poisson, $\varepsilon_0 = 1$:

$$
\phi'' = -\rho, \qquad E = -\phi'
$$

Periodic, so the mean of $\rho$ has to vanish or there is no solution. A uniform ion background cancels the mean electron density. Ions do not move. That is the whole ion model in this lesson.

## Four beats

One step, in this order.

The hats are linear, support one cell. Cloud-in-cell, 1969:

$$
W_i(x) = 1 - \frac{|x - x_i|}{h}
\quad\text{on a support of }h
$$

$$
\rho_i = \frac{1}{h}\sum_p q_p W_i(x_p),
\qquad
E_p = \sum_i E_i W_i(x_p)
$$

One particle, one cell of width 1. Left node gets $1-x$, right node gets $x$.

Matching is why gather is the adjoint of scatter.

$$
\sum_p q_p\,\phi_p = \sum_i \rho_i\,\phi_i\,h
$$

A lone particle on a uniform mesh, with a neutralizing background, then feels no self-force: the force identity $\sum q_p E_p = 0$ is a two-entry version of the same dot product. Use different weights to gather than you used to deposit and a particle accelerates in its own cloud.

## A cold sea rings

$N$ electrons, $q_p = -1/N$, $m_p = 1/N$, box of length 1. Then $\omega_p = 1$. Displace them by a small $k=1$ sine and let go.

The lab measures that period from the PIC run. Continuum Langmuir is $2\pi/\omega_p$. The grid is close, because $k\Delta x$ is small. Switch to **two-stream**: two beams, same Poisson, and the field bunches them instead of ringing. Instability is not a different method.

<Recall id="pic-one-line" concept="particle-in-cell">
  <div slot="front">Where does charge live, where does the field live, and why is there no N² loop?</div>

Charge lives on **particles**. The field lives on a **mesh**. Deposit scatters $q_p$ onto $\rho_i$ with linear hats, Poisson solves $E$ from $\rho$, gather interpolates $E$ with the same hats, and leapfrog pushes the particles. The Poisson solve *is* the interaction — every charge talks to every other charge through the grid, so you never name the pairs.

</Recall>

<Tier level="advanced" title="self-force, grid heating, and the 1D honesty">

Matching hats kill self-force on a uniform periodic mesh. Mismatch them — NGP deposit, CIC gather — and a lone particle accelerates in its own cloud. The adjoint identity above is the test: if $\sum q\phi_p$ fails to equal $\sum \rho\phi\,h$, the interpolation is wrong.

**Grid heating.** Particle noise aliases onto grid modes. If $\Delta x$ is coarser than the Debye length, those aliases can be unstable and the plasma heats without a physical collision operator. The finite-grid instability is why PIC papers quote $\Delta x \lesssim \lambda_D$ and a CFL-like $\omega_p\Delta t \lesssim 0.2$. This lesson's Langmuir run sits inside both.

**1D is too nice.** On a line, exact electrostatics is a prefix sum after a sort: $E$ jumps by $q$ at each particle. That is $\Order(N\log N)$, not $\Order(N^2)$, and it is not PIC. PIC is the three-dimensional algorithm, practised in 1D so the field is a picture. In 3D the Poisson solve (FFT, multigrid, or a tree) is why the method exists.

Harlow's 1957/64 PIC scattered mass for fluids. Brackbill's FLIP kept the particle increment so plasmas stopped cooling. Sulsky's MPM puts stress on the particles and throws the grid away every step. Same transfer, different physics.

</Tier>

## What to carry forward

Charge as clouds on a mesh. The field is a Poisson solve, not a sum over pairs.

Deposit and gather use the same hats, or a particle feels itself. Refine particles to kill noise; refine the grid to resolve the field.
