Rest was $f_i = w_i\rho$ with $w = \{\tfrac16,\tfrac23,\tfrac16\}$. Collision is what rebuilds that cancellation after streaming has moved the pieces.

$$
f_i^\star = f_i + \tfrac{1}{\tau}\bigl(f_i^{\mathrm{eq}}(\rho,u) - f_i\bigr),
\qquad
f_i(x, t+1) = f_i^\star(x - e_i)
$$

$\rho$ and $u$ are moments of the populations you already have. You never stored a fluid.

$$
\rho = \sum_i f_i, \qquad \rho u = \sum_i e_i f_i
$$

A [partial differential equation](../../fields-and-continua/from-particles-to-fields/) for a fluid is an equation for those moments. Lattice Boltzmann never discretises it. The state is $f_i$. Navier–Stokes is what the moments look like after enough of this map.

<Recall id="lbm-stream-collide-moments" concept="lattice-boltzmann">
  <div slot="front">What does lattice Boltzmann store, what do the two operators do, and how do you get density and velocity?</div>

The state is discrete-velocity populations $f_i$. **Stream** shifts each $f_i$ along its lattice velocity $e_i$ (bounce-back at a wall). **Collide** relaxes $f$ toward $f^{\mathrm{eq}}(\rho,u)$ with rate $1/\tau$. Density and velocity are moments, $\rho=\sum f_i$ and $\rho u=\sum e_i f_i$ — readouts, not the variables you stepped. Navier–Stokes is recovered from that map, not discretised.

</Recall>

## A channel that was never a velocity field

Chapman–Enskog — a multi-scale expansion of the discrete Boltzmann equation — is the proof that those moments satisfy Navier–Stokes with kinematic viscosity

$$
\nu = c_s^2\bigl(\tau - \tfrac12\bigr) = \tfrac13\bigl(\tau - \tfrac12\bigr).
$$

Doubling $(\tau-\tfrac12)$ doubles $\nu$ and halves the mid-channel speed. Hunt that.

<Tier level="advanced" title="Chapman–Enskog, Mach, and when this is a costume">

The equilibrium is a second-order Hermite expansion of a Maxwellian, truncated at $u^2$:

$$
f_i^{\mathrm{eq}} = w_i\rho\Bigl(1 + \frac{e_i\cdot u}{c_s^2} + \frac{(e_i\cdot u)^2}{2c_s^4} - \frac{u^2}{2c_s^2}\Bigr).
$$

Chapman–Enskog expands $f_i = f_i^{\mathrm{eq}} + \varepsilon f_i^{(1)}+\cdots$ in the Knudsen number. The $\varepsilon^0$ solvability condition is mass and momentum conservation (which BGK is built to satisfy). The $\varepsilon^1$ condition is Navier–Stokes, with that viscosity $\nu=c_s^2(\tau-\tfrac12)$ and a bulk viscosity that LBM does not let you pick independently of $\tau$ — one of the reasons MRT and regularised LBM exist.

The truncation is why **Mach** is a wall. $u$ has to stay $\ll c_s$ or the missing $u^3$ terms in $f^{\mathrm{eq}}$ are no longer small, and you are simulating a weakly compressible gas with the wrong equilibrium, not incompressible NS. The demo channel at $\tau=1$ runs at $\mathrm{Ma}\sim 0.03$ on purpose.

Halfway bounce-back is second-order for this Poiseuille (the walls sit on the links; the error in $u$ is a known $\tau$-dependent shift). For a curved body it drops to first order unless you interpolate (Bouzidi, Filippova–Hänel). The lattice-gas ancestor (FHP) had the same velocities and a discrete collision table; LBM is what you get when occupation numbers become real $f_i$ and the collision becomes linear.

When LBM is a good CFD engine: low-Ma incompressible flow, complex bounce-back geometry, trivial parallelism (collide is local, stream is a neighbour shift). When it is a costume: high Mach, some thermal problems, any BC that is not naturally a statement about populations. Then you are wearing kinetic clothing over a PDE you should have discretised.

</Tier>

## What to carry forward

- The state is $f_i$. Stream along lattice links; collide toward $f^{\mathrm{eq}}$; $\rho$ and $u$ are moments.
- Viscosity is a collision fact: $\nu=c_s^2(\tau-\tfrac12)$. CFL is already 1 — you do not pick $\Delta t$.
- Navier–Stokes is recovered from this map, not stored. If you needed a different PDE, this is the wrong gas.
